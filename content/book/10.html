Primer

No one builds a business alone for long. At some point, the business is only as good as the people in it and how well they work together. That makes hiring, team design and leadership some of the most important decisions an entrepreneur makes.

People problems are also where chaos spreads fastest. A wrong hire, a team that keeps changing, unclear authority or a disagreement nobody says out loud can do more damage than a bad product. The seven rules in this section are about choosing people, keeping teams small and steady, making authority clear, building the culture you want from the first day, and leading by setting the goal rather than every step.

The main story comes from my years as an intrapreneur, when I built new digital teams inside manufacturers, including centers in Pakistan and in Bangalore, India. In line with this book’s rule, the companies and the people are not named.

Key terms

  • Team. A small group with a shared goal, shared responsibility for the result and members who depend on each other (Hackman, 2002).
  • Authority. The right to decide. Clear authority means everyone knows who decides what.
  • Culture. The shared habits and unwritten rules of a group, which shape how its members work.
  • Center of excellence. A team, often in another location, built to be expert at one kind of work and to serve the whole company.
  • Psychological safety. A shared belief that it is safe to speak up, ask questions and admit mistakes (Edmondson, 1999).
  • Task conflict vs. relationship conflict. Disagreement about the work, which can help, vs. personal conflict, which hurts (Jehn, 1995).

The Rules

83 · Every hire changes the company you become Hunger

Each person you hire changes what your company can do and what it becomes. Treat hiring as one of your most important decisions, and keep the bar high. “People like us” is not a bar.

  • Employment blueprint: when young companies changed their early model of whom to hire and how to manage them, employee turnover rose, and that turnover hurt later performance (Baron et al., 2001).

84 · Small, steady teams beat big, changing ones Chaos · Hunger

Each extra person adds less work and more coordination (Brooks, 1975). Keep teams small, keep the same people together, and make them a real team (Hackman, 2002). Add someone new from time to time, on purpose, so the team keeps hearing new ideas.

  • Team familiarity: teams whose members have worked together before tend to perform better, while the number of years each person had spent at the firm was not consistently linked to performance (Huckman et al., 2009).

→ See Building the first center.

85 · Flat teams still need someone clearly in charge Chaos

Removing bosses does not remove power. It hides it (Freeman, 1972–1973). Make authority clear: write down which role decides what, and give each decision to whoever the task needs (Lee & Edmondson, 2017).

86 · Your first habits become a culture nobody can copy Hunger

Early values turn into shared habits, and habits turn into rules (Schein, 1983). Rivals can copy your tools, but not easily that culture (Barney, 1986). A strong culture is also hard to change, so build the one you will want later.

87 · Hidden disagreement hurts more than an open argument Chaos · Curiosity

Silence is not agreement (Morrison & Milliken, 2000). Build a team that can argue openly about the work while keeping respect (Jehn, 1995). Set a time to argue, and a time to commit.

→ See When silence is not agreement.

88 · Your partner matters as much as your idea Curiosity

Great builders rarely work alone. The people you work with shape your results and how others judge your work (Rossman et al., 2010). Write down early who does what, who owns what, and how either partner can leave.

89 · Set the goal; let the team find the way Curiosity · Hunger

An old management saying holds that people support what they help build. Be firm on what to achieve, open on how to achieve it, and invite the people closest to the work. Safety and legal rules are never open.

  • Compelling direction: teams need clear ends, with the means left to them (Hackman, 2002).

→ See The goal, not the steps.

The Story: Building Teams Far From Home

Before the teams

My first experience leading people was not in business. From 2004 to 2005, I was a team leader for a home-building charity in Phoenix, building houses with volunteers. We built five houses. Volunteers are a hard team to lead: you cannot pay them more and you cannot fire them. The only tools you have are a clear goal, a good plan for the day and respect.

In 2006, I spent a summer teaching English full-time in Harbin, China. I learned to be understood across a language and a culture, and to check whether people had understood me rather than assuming they had.

Both lessons came back years later, when I had to build teams in countries far from home.

Why build a team far away

After my startup years, I spent more than a decade as an intrapreneur, building new digital businesses inside established companies. From 2017 to 2020, two of those roles asked me to do something new: build a digital team from scratch in another country.

The first was at a U.S. manufacturer, where I was Director of Ecommerce from 2017 to 2019. I built a digital center of excellence in Pakistan.

The second was at a global consumer products group, where I was Senior Director of Global Digital Marketing and Ecommerce from 2019 to 2020. I built a digital center of excellence in Bangalore, India, and I was responsible for the profit and loss of the global ecommerce division.

Across these and other roles, I trained and led digital marketing and ecommerce teams of up to 40 people, working across more than 15 countries.

Pause and guess: Why would a company build a team in another country instead of hiring at home?

Building the first center

When you build a team from nothing, every early choice becomes a habit, and every habit becomes the culture (Rule 86). So the first question is not “how many people?” It is “which few people, working together for a long time?”

Rule 84 says small, steady teams beat big, changing ones. A team that stays together learns how each person works, who is strong at what, and how to hand work across without losing it. That matters even more when the team is in a different time zone from the business it serves, because every handover across that distance is a chance for something to be lost.

The division I led grew 86% in its first year.

The goal, not the steps

Pause and guess: Write one goal for a team without telling them how to reach it.

A leader far away cannot manage every step. The time difference alone makes it impossible. So the leader’s job changes. You become firm on the goal, clear on the standards that are never negotiable, and open on how the team reaches the goal (Rule 89).

That is also how a remote team grows. If people only follow instructions, the team can never be better than the instructions. If people own the “how,” they can find a better way than the one you had in mind.

When silence is not agreement

Leading across cultures taught me that silence can mean many things. It can mean agreement. It can also mean confusion, politeness, respect for the boss or disagreement that does not feel safe to say (Rule 87).

In a meeting across countries and languages, a quiet room is not proof that a plan is good. The leader has to make it safe, and normal, to argue about the work.

Pause and guess: In your family, school or workplace, what does silence in a meeting usually mean?

Owning the numbers

In Bangalore, the role came with something new: full responsibility for the profit and loss of the global ecommerce division. That changes how you lead people. Every hire, every tool and every campaign becomes a decision about the business, not only about the team. In that role, the division I led grew 200%.

What I would tell a founder in your city

Most students reading this will not build a team in another country next year. But many will build a team of three or five people, often family or friends. The same rules apply.

Choose the first few people with great care, because they will shape everything after them (Rule 83). Keep the team small and steady (Rule 84). Make it safe to disagree, and ask the quietest person first (Rule 87). And tell people clearly what you need to achieve, then let them show you how (Rule 89).
