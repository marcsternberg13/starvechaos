Primer

An opportunity is a need that nobody is serving well yet, plus a way for you to serve it that someone will pay for. Ideas are everywhere. Opportunities are rarer, because an opportunity has a buyer.

Most people look for opportunities by thinking hard about new ideas. Entrepreneurs usually find them another way: by watching. They watch what people actually do, which rules an industry follows without asking why, which customers nobody serves, and which rules are changing right now. The nine rules in this section are nine places to look.

The main story is how I started selling on Amazon in 2003, before I had a company, because I could see a door opening that most businesses had not noticed yet.

Key terms

  • Idea. A possible product or service. It costs nothing and proves nothing.
  • Opportunity. A need, a buyer who will pay to meet it, and a way for you to meet it better than the current option.
  • Assumption. Something an industry believes without checking. Many openings hide inside assumptions.
  • Gap. A need that every current supplier misses.
  • Platform. A marketplace or system where many buyers and sellers meet, such as an online store that lets other businesses sell through it.
  • Early mover. A business that enters while the rules are still being written. Being early is not the same as being first (see Part 3, Phrase 7).

The Rules

13 · List the rules everyone follows, then break one Curiosity · Chaos

Every industry runs on rules that nobody questions. Write them down, then ask what happens if one of them is false. The rule everyone accepts is often where customers are least happy.

  • Not every rule is waste: some exist for safety, law or trust. Break the ones that only protect habit.

→ See The rule nobody questioned.

14 · The customer everyone ignores is often the opening Curiosity · Hunger

Look for people an industry overlooks or leaves out. If a group spends money but no one serves it well, that group is an open market.

  • Check they can pay: sometimes a group is ignored because serving it costs more than it earns.

15 · Watch what people actually use, not what experts predict Curiosity

What people really do tells you more than what experts forecast. Study what customers install, fix, hack and change without being asked. Their behavior shows needs that no survey will find.

  • Lead users: customers who feel a need before the rest of the market, and often build their own fix (von Hippel, 1986).

→ See A customer before a seller.

16 · Boring or worthless-looking things can hide real money Hunger · Curiosity

You do not need a glamorous idea. Something other people call dull, or even free, can become a business if someone needs it and no one is supplying it well.

17 · When the rules change, a new door opens Chaos · Curiosity

New platforms, laws and reward systems create new openings. While the rules are still being written, the people who move early can learn them first and help shape them.

  • Platform risk: the same change that opens a door can close it. If your business depends on one platform or one law, plan for the next change.

→ See Why early mattered.

18 · Your next opening may already be in your drawer Curiosity · Hunger

Before you search outside, look at what you already have. A side tool, an old project or a skill you take for granted can become the main business.

19 · Start with someone’s real problem, not a shiny tool Curiosity

Good openings come from a pain people feel or a limit they keep hitting. A new technology with no clear problem is a tool looking for a use, and it usually finds no buyer.

  • Bottleneck: the one step that slows everything else. Problems often hide there.

20 · Map what everyone offers; the gap is yours Curiosity · Hunger

List what each competitor offers and what each one misses. The space all of them leave empty is your opening, if a real customer is standing in it.

21 · Many good openings are borrowed from somewhere else Curiosity

An idea that works in one place, industry or country can often work in another. Teams often do better work when they bring in knowledge from outside the company (Haas, 2010). Test the borrowed idea locally before you bet on it.

The Story: Getting On Early

A customer before a seller

I did not discover Amazon as a business. I discovered it as a customer. I used it to buy books, like many people did in the early 2000s.

Then I noticed something. Amazon had started to open categories beyond books. But when I looked inside those new categories, there was not much there. The shelves were thin. A company that had built its name on books was now inviting other products in, and most businesses had not shown up yet.

That is Rule 15 in action. I was not reading forecasts about online retail. I was using the product, and the product was showing me where it was going. The empty shelves were the signal.

Why early mattered

I was sure Amazon would become something very big. I did not know exactly how big, but I knew I wanted to be on it early, for two reasons.

The first was learning. When a platform is new, its rules are still being written (Rule 17). How do products get ranked? What makes a listing sell? What do customers trust? Nobody has the answers yet, so the people who are there early learn them first. By the time the crowd arrives, the early sellers already know how the system works.

The second reason was the products themselves. I wanted to bring products and brands onto the digital shelf before anyone else did. In a store, a brand fights for physical shelf space. Online, the shelf was almost empty, and whoever filled it first had a real advantage.

So in 2003, I started selling. I did not have a company yet. I simply started listing products and learning.

The rule nobody questioned

At the time, most brands sold through stores. Shoppers went to a store, and the store bought from the brand or from a wholesaler (a business that buys in large amounts from makers and sells to stores). That was the rule everyone followed, and almost nobody asked whether it had to be that way (Rule 13).

I broke that rule in a simple way. I looked for products that were already selling well in stores but were not yet listed on Amazon. I bought them, and I listed them myself. The product had already proved itself to shoppers. What it was missing was a place on the new shelf.

This was not a new invention. It was an old product in a new place. That is often what an opening looks like: nothing in the product changes, but where and how it is sold does.

Pause and guess: If you wanted to find products that sell well in stores but are missing online, where would you look first?

Learning before forming

For two years, from 2003 to 2005, I sold without forming a company. That was on purpose. I wanted to learn how the platform worked before I committed to building something bigger on top of it.

Those two years taught me what sold and what did not, which listings customers trusted, and how the rankings moved. They also taught me what I could not see from the outside. Some products I was sure about went nowhere. Others surprised me.

In 2005, once I understood the platform, I formed a company for this work. It ran until 2010. Over those years I introduced many brands to Amazon, including fashion, jewelry and wearable brands, and helped them build their own storefronts on the site.

Two products that showed the way

My biggest successes were in two categories that nobody would call exciting: candles and laundry detergent.

With the candles, I became friends with the owner of the company that made them. Together we got the products ranked very high on the site. The candles sold so well that Amazon started buying from the company directly. I had set the brand up and made money doing it. Then Amazon saw the opportunity, cut me out and went straight to the maker, which also owned the brand. That was fine. I went on to work with several other candle companies and many candle brands throughout my career.

With the detergent, the story ended differently. The detergent company moved to another state and shifted toward research and heavy commercial work. It stopped making the home products I had been selling so well. A product that had been working for me disappeared because of a decision made by someone else.

I tell the full selling side of these two stories in Section 08. Here, notice the opportunity lesson. Both openings came from ordinary products, not glamorous ones. And the detergent showed me that an opening you do not control can close at any time (Rule 17, and Rule 3 in Section 01). It also led me somewhere new: that detergent connection is how I ended up in the laundry business (Sections 01 and 05).

What changed, and what stayed

Amazon did become very large, just as I had expected. As it grew, the rules changed. Being early gave me years of learning that later sellers had to pay for. But early is never permanent. Every platform keeps rewriting its rules, and each change helps some sellers and hurts others.

Pause and guess: If you build a business on someone else’s platform, what happens when they change the rules?

When I look for openings today, in film or anywhere else, I still ask the same three questions I asked in 2003. Where am I already a customer, and what am I noticing? Which rule does everyone in this industry follow without asking why? And what is changing right now, so that the rules are still being written?

Ask those questions about your own city. Which apps, payment systems or trade routes are new? Which shelves, online or in the bazaar, are still empty?
