Primer

An entrepreneur looks at a business the way an owner does. The owner carries the whole result, good or bad. Nobody else will fix what the owner ignores. That simple fact changes how you see everything: goals, values, competitors, mistakes and the industry you work in.

This first section is about that outlook. It comes before any tool or method, because tools used without an owner’s outlook produce busy people and weak results. The twelve rules here are the habits of mind that the rest of the book builds on.

The main story in this section is my commercial laundry company. It is not a glamorous business. That is the point. An owner’s outlook can turn a dull industry into a strong company.

Key terms

  • Owner. The person who carries the result of the business, including the blame. You can be an owner without owning shares. An employee who acts this way is an intrapreneur (see Part 1).
  • Goal. Where you want to be, such as “double sales in two years.” A goal says nothing about how.
  • Strategy. A small set of choices about who you serve, what you offer and why customers pick you. A good strategy tells people what to do without asking the boss.
  • Trade-off. A choice where doing more of one thing means doing less of another. Real strategy is full of trade-offs (Porter, 1996).
  • Structure. The rules, rewards, roles and money flows around people. Structure shapes behavior more than personality does.
  • Value (as in “our values”). A belief the business will not trade away, even for profit.

The Rules

1 · A goal is a wish; strategy is a few choices Hunger · Curiosity

A big goal does not tell anyone what to do on Monday morning. A strategy does: it turns the goal into a small set of core choices that let each person decide alone (Van den Steen, 2017). A clear strategy statement names the objective, the scope and the advantage (Collis & Rukstad, 2008). Test each choice by asking whether a rival could copy it word for word. If one could, it is not yet a choice.

2 · Values decide which options you even consider Hunger · Chaos

In a business led by its values, values work like walls, not weights. Some profitable options are removed before anyone adds up the numbers. Research on what Tetlock et al. (2000) call sacred values shows the same pattern: people refuse even to weigh some options against money. This saves time and keeps the business true to its purpose under pressure.

  • Wall, not weight: a weight is balanced against money; a wall is never put on the scale.

→ See Choosing who we would serve.

3 · Own the thing that can shut you down Hunger · Chaos

Find out who can stop your work: a landlord, a supplier, a platform, a single big customer. Then either own that piece yourself, or plan only at the level you truly control. Everything else is a risk you carry without seeing it.

4 · Take the blame so your people take risks Hunger · Chaos

People try bold things when they believe a failure will not destroy them, a shared belief researchers call psychological safety (Edmondson, 1999). The owner absorbs the blame and the uncertainty, so the team can take risks without fear.

  • Not the same as no standards: a bold experiment that fails is accepted; careless work is not (Pisano, 2019).

5 · It looks personal, but it is usually structural Curiosity · Chaos

When people behave badly, the cause is often the system around them, not their character. Before you blame a person, check the rules, the rewards (Kerr, 1975), the shared task, and who approves, pays, benefits and carries the risk.

6 · Being better is not the same as being different Chaos · Hunger

When every business copies the same best practice, they all become alike, and customers collect the gains through lower prices. Owners choose to do different things, not the same things harder.

  • Operational effectiveness: doing the same activities better than rivals. Necessary, but rivals can copy it (Porter, 1996).
  • Strategic position: doing different activities, or the same ones in a different way, so you are not compared on price alone (Porter, 1996).

→ See Different, not better.

7 · Ask what the business lacks, then buy, borrow or build it Hunger · Curiosity

What you spend money on, not what you say, is your real strategy. Owners look honestly at what the company is missing, then put money where the gap is.

8 · Owners ask for help and keep the responsibility Curiosity · Hunger

Owning a result does not mean knowing every answer. Owners design the system, ask experts and users for solutions, and still carry the result themselves.

9 · Change never ends, so stop waiting for normal Chaos · Hunger

Change is the normal state of a business, not a break from it. Owners keep adjusting as they go instead of waiting for things to settle. A better question than “When will things go back to normal?” is “What are we adjusting this week?”

10 · Your real strategy is what you do each day Chaos · Curiosity

Plans are rewritten by the people who carry them out. The daily choices about time and money are the real strategy, so watch those choices and learn from them.

  • Intended and emergent strategy: the plan you wrote, and the pattern that appears from what people actually do (Mintzberg & Waters, 1985). The real strategy mixes both.

11 · Decide with most of the facts, not all of them Hunger · Chaos

Waiting for certainty is also a decision, and usually a costly one. Owners decide once they know enough, explain why, and fix the choice if it proves wrong. Jeff Bezos, Amazon’s founder, argues that most decisions should be made with about 70% of the information you wish you had (Bezos, 2017).

  • Two-way door: a decision you can reverse. Decide these fast (Bezos, 2016).
  • One-way door: a decision you cannot reverse, such as a long lease or a large loan. Slow down for these (Bezos, 2016).

12 · The company you build matters more than the industry you pick Hunger · Curiosity

A hard or boring industry is not a life sentence. How you run your company explains much more of its profit than the industry it sits in. In one large study, the industry explained about 8% of the differences in profit between businesses; the business itself explained about 36% (Vanneste, 2017).

→ See A boring industry on purpose.

Rule 93 · Change one piece and the other pieces must change — see Section 11.

The Story: Three Times the Price for White Towels

How I ended up in laundry

I did not dream about laundry. I got there sideways. In my early Amazon business, one of my best-selling products was a laundry detergent (that story is in Section 02). Through that detergent company, I started to see the laundry business from the inside: who buys, who washes, and who gets paid.

Then I asked my university, Arizona State, a simple question: what are your biggest contracts? The answer led me to laundry. The full story of that question, and the rule the university gave me, is in Section 05. Here I want to tell you what happened after I decided to build a laundry company, and what it taught me about thinking like an owner.

A boring industry on purpose

Commercial laundry is a hard business. It is heavy, wet and physical. It has trucks, machines, chemicals and early mornings. Prices are quoted by the pound, which tells you something: customers see it as a commodity, a product where one supplier is as good as another.

Most people would call that a bad industry to enter. I saw it differently, and Rule 12 explains why. The industry sets the floor and the ceiling, but most of the difference between a good laundry company and a bad one comes from how it is run: which customers it chooses, what it promises, how it prices and how reliably it delivers.

There was also less competition for attention. Few young founders wanted to wash towels. That meant fewer smart rivals and more room to do something different.

My business plan for the company won the Kauffman Foundation’s Entrepreneurial Advantage Award in 2008. I was proud of that. But an award for a plan is not the same as a business that works. The plan only became real when the trucks were full.

Choosing who we would serve

The most important decision I made was not about machines. It was about customers. I built the company around high-end businesses that cared about the environment and about social responsibility: boutique hotels, spas, gyms, offices and other premium places. Most of what we washed was small white towels.

That choice worked like a wall (Rule 2). It removed whole groups of possible customers before we ever discussed price. A business that only wanted the cheapest wash was not our customer, even if it was large and even if it would pay on time. We did not weigh that business against the others. It was simply outside the wall.

This felt risky at first. Saying no to money is hard when you are young and the business is small. But the wall did three things for us. It told us which customers to call. It told us what to say to them. And it told us which promises we could keep, because we only had to be excellent for one kind of customer.

Pause and guess: Why would a green spa care how its towels are washed?

Different, not better

Here is the number people find hard to believe. I charged about three times my competitors’ price per pound.

If I had tried to be a better ordinary laundry, with slightly whiter towels or slightly faster trucks, I could never have charged three times the price. Better is easy to copy. Every rival can buy the same machine or promise the same speed, and then the customer simply picks the cheapest one (Rule 6).

So I did not compete on the same terms. I chose a different customer, with different needs, and built the service around them. A boutique hotel or a spa sells an experience. A towel is part of that experience. A green and socially responsible spa also wants a supplier whose way of working matches the story it tells its own guests. For that customer, the cheapest laundry is not the best value. The right laundry is.

That is what being different means. It is not a slogan. It is a different set of customers, a different promise and a different price, all fitting together.

Pause and guess: What could a laundry offer that is worth three times the price to a boutique hotel?

A truckload a day

The model worked. Soon we were handling a truckload of laundry every day. At that volume, I no longer needed the big university contract that had first drawn me into laundry. I began bidding on large hotels instead.

This is the part of the story where the owner’s outlook matters most. Growth tempts you to drop your walls. A large hotel may want a lower price. A big new customer may not care about the environment at all. Each decision looks small. Together, they can turn a different company back into an ordinary one.

Pause and guess: A big hotel offers you a large contract at half your price. Do you take it? What does it do to the wall around your customers?

Selling at 23

I sold the laundry company when I was 23. It was the first business I sold, and it taught me something no class did: a company is worth what someone else will pay for the thing you built, not for the effort you put in.

What I took with me

I have used three lessons from that company in every venture since.

First, choose the company you will build before you choose the industry. People asked me why I would wash towels. The better question was how I would run a towel business that no one else could copy. (Rule 12.)

Second, let your values remove options early. The wall around our customers did more for the business than any spreadsheet. It made the hard choices once, so we did not have to make them again every week. (Rule 2.)

Third, do not try to be better at the same thing. Try to be different in a way the right customer will pay for. Three times the price per pound is not possible for a better laundry. It is possible for a different one. (Rule 6.)

When you look at a business in your city, whether it is a bakery, a phone repair stall or a small workshop, ask the same three questions. Who is this business for? What will it refuse? And what does it do differently, not just better?
