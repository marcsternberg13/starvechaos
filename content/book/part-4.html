What starving chaos looks like on a Monday

This book began with one sentence: failure is not chaos; chaos is chaos. After 101 rules, here is what that sentence looks like in practice.

It looks like a founder who knows which customer the business is for, and which customers it will refuse. Who can name the one cost that turns a good-looking product into a loss. Who has written down, before the test, what result would prove the idea wrong. Who has checked whether all of the business’s bets depend on the same buyer, the same supplier or the same future. Who knows exactly who decides what on the team, and has made it safe for people to disagree.

None of that removes uncertainty. Customers will still surprise you. Suppliers will still move to another state. Platforms will still rewrite their rules. Some of what you build will still fail. But a business run this way fails in ways it understands, at a price it can afford, and learns something every time. That is the difference between failure and chaos.

Hunger, curiosity and chaos, together

The three forces in the title need each other.

Hunger without curiosity builds fast in the wrong direction. It chases every customer, adds every product, spends on patents before software, and calls the result growth.

Curiosity without hunger studies the market forever and never sells. It runs one more test, asks one more question and waits for one more fact.

And both of them, without respect for chaos, assume the world will hold still long enough for the plan to work. It will not.

The rules are how you balance the three. Hunger gives you the drive to start. Curiosity turns that drive into evidence. And a clear view of chaos tells you which risks to accept and which to starve.

What my own life taught me

When I look back across more than 15 businesses, a decade of building inside other companies and now a slate of about 30 film projects, the lessons that mattered most are not complicated.

Ask where the money already is, the way I asked my university about its biggest contracts. Get on the platform before it is obvious, the way I did on Amazon in 2003. Charge for the result, not the hour, the way I learned with my landscaping customers. Choose who you serve and be different for them, the way I did with the laundry. Spend first on proving the business, not protecting it, the lesson my patents taught me. And when you start something new, make the first bet small enough to survive, the way I started in film.

None of those required genius, a garage or a large investment. Each required paying attention, asking a question, and being willing to be wrong in a way I could afford.

Going back to the rules

This is a dictionary. It is not meant to be read once and put away. Come back to it when you face a decision.

Before you start something, read Sections 02 and 03: where is the opening, and what do you already have? Before you commit money, read Sections 04 and 09: can you survive this bet, and do you know your numbers? When something goes wrong, read Section 06: what died, what survived, and what did it teach? When the business starts to grow, read Sections 07, 10 and 11: what will you say no to, who will you build with, and which step is now the slowest?

And when someone gives you confident advice in a single sentence, check Part 3 first.

Five questions for your next decision

If you remember nothing else from this book, ask these five questions before any important decision.

  1. Who is this for, and who is it not for? (Rules 2 and 60.)
  2. What would have to be true for this to work, and which of those am I least sure of? (Rules 41 and 42.)
  3. What can I afford to lose, and when will I stop? (Rules 30 and 38.)
  4. What does this cost all the way down to my pocket? (Rule 75.)
  5. What will I learn, whatever happens? (Rule 53.)

If you can answer all five, you are not removing chaos. You are starving it.
