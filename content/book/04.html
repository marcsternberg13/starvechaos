Primer

Entrepreneurs are often described as people who love risk. That is not quite right (see Part 3, Phrase 6). Good entrepreneurs accept risk, but they shape it. They make many small bets instead of one large one. They check whether their bets can all fail at the same time. They decide in advance when they will walk away. And they remember that doing nothing is also a bet.

This section is about deciding when you cannot know the future. You will never have all the facts. The goal is not to remove uncertainty, which is impossible, but to place bets you can survive and learn from. That is the heart of starving chaos: accept the risk you understand, and refuse the chaos you do not.

The main story is how I moved from ecommerce into film, and how I learned that a slate of many different film projects can still be one big bet.

Key terms

  • Risk. A danger whose odds you can roughly estimate, such as the chance a customer pays late.
  • Uncertainty. A situation where you cannot even estimate the odds, such as what your industry will look like in five years. This split between risk and uncertainty comes from Knight (1921).
  • Bet. Any commitment of money, time or reputation whose result you cannot know in advance.
  • Portfolio. A group of bets held together. In film, a producer’s group of projects is called a slate.
  • Correlated bets. Bets that tend to win or lose together because they depend on the same thing.
  • Scenario. A believable story about one possible future, used to test a plan. Scenarios are not forecasts.
  • Stop rule. A limit, set before you start, that tells you when to quit.

The Rules

34 · Many different bets can still fail together Chaos · Curiosity

Bets that look different can depend on the same money, the same buyers or the same belief about the future. If one shock can hit them all at once, you have one big bet, not many small ones. Spread your bets across different funders, buyers and assumptions.

  • Variety is not safety: what lowers risk is how differently your bets behave, not how many you hold (Markowitz, 1952).

→ See Seven projects, one future.

35 · Prepare for several futures, not one forecast Chaos

As one foresight researcher puts it, “there are no facts about the future” (Wilkinson, 2017, p. 3). So do not bet everything on one forecast. Picture several believable futures and check that your plan can survive in each before you commit.

  • Plausible, not probable: a scenario only needs to be believable and consistent, not likely.

→ See Four futures.

36 · Bet on the things that will not change Chaos · Hunger

When the future is unclear, build on what customers will still want in ten years: fair prices, speed, trust, a good story. Work on lasting wants keeps paying off whichever future arrives. Jeff Bezos made this argument about Amazon: he could not imagine customers ten years later wanting higher prices, less selection or slower delivery (Bezos, 2009).

37 · Test the new life before you leave the old one Hunger · Curiosity

Make your first bet small and safe. Prove the new work at a small scale while you still have income, then grow it step by step.

  • Too long is also a risk: some ideas need full attention to work. Set a date or a signal that tells you it is time to jump.

→ See Testing the new life.

38 · Decide when you will walk away before you start Chaos · Hunger

Set your top price and your stop point before you begin. Once you are in a deal, pride and effort make it hard to quit. A limit set in advance protects you and frees money and time for the next bet.

  • Escalation of commitment: the habit of putting more into a failing course of action because you have already invested so much (Staw, 1976).

39 · Good bets often look foolish at first Curiosity · Chaos

New things usually start out worse than the old ones, and people laugh at them. Judge a new idea by how fast it is improving, not only by how good it is today. Many products that later beat the market leaders started out this way (Christensen, 1997). Most ideas that look foolish are foolish; look for real signs of fast improvement.

→ See Why it looked foolish.

40 · Standing still is also a bet Chaos

Doing nothing feels safe, but it is a choice with its own risks. When you compare options, always count the cost of keeping things as they are.

  • Status quo bias: people choose the current option more often than they should (Samuelson & Zeckhauser, 1988).

41 · Ask what would have to be true Curiosity

Arguing about whether an idea is right rarely ends well. Instead, list what must be true for it to work (Lafley & Martin, 2013). Then focus on the few conditions you doubt most, and check those first (also in Section 05).

The Story: Thirty Bets

Testing the new life

I have loved film since I was a child. I made film projects with friends. We tried to get them into festivals. I was obsessed with every part of it: filming, editing, directing and writing.

But for about twenty years, my career was in ecommerce and digital marketing. I built online sales and teams inside other companies, and I ran my own growth consulting practice beside every job. Film stayed on the side.

When I started to think seriously about film, I did not quit my job and jump. I tested first (Rule 37). While I was still working in ecommerce, I studied screenwriting at the University of Oxford, from 2022 to 2024. In 2023, I completed a producing and entertainment executive certificate. I have written 14 screenplays, and I have produced or executive produced more than 200 commercials and pieces of brand content, which taught me how to run a shoot, a budget and a crew on a small scale.

Each of these was a small bet. None of them could ruin me. Each one told me something: whether I still loved the work when it was hard, whether I was any good at it, and whether my business skills transferred.

Why it looked foolish

After my last ecommerce role, I did a lot of soul-searching. Then I chose film and television as my next business, a field where I had never worked full time.

From the outside, this looked foolish (Rule 39). I was leaving an industry where I had twenty years of results for one where my past wins did not count. Film projects usually take three to five years, more often five. They need a lot of money. Most never get made.

But I did not see it as starting over. A film producer is the entrepreneur of a film. The producer raises the money, signs the contracts, pays everyone and is responsible for the whole project, which can involve hundreds of people and a very large budget. Every skill I had built, in selling, finance, teams and launches, was a producing skill. What I lacked was the language of the industry and how it works. So I went and learned it: I earned a master’s degree in producing at Chapman University, finishing in 2026, and made several films while in the program.

When you judge a bet that looks foolish, ask what is improving fast. In my case it was my knowledge of the field, my network and the quality of my projects.

Pause and guess: Would you leave a twenty-year career for a field where your past wins do not count? What would you need to see first?

The first bet: one documentary

I founded my production company, Son of a Botanist Productions, in 2023. I did not start by raising money from investors. I started with one documentary that I paid for entirely myself.

I began with eight contacts and followed the leads. That grew to 200 contacts, and I interviewed 50 people. I did every job myself: producer, director, camera operator and editor. I did it because I had to. There was no budget for anyone else.

That was a deliberately small bet (Rule 37 again). If the film had gone nowhere, I would have lost my own money and my own time, but nothing else. And because I did the work before I asked anyone for money, I had something real to show. That finished work convinced an investor to help me complete the project. The same investor then invested in my next project. (Showing the work before you ask is Rule 66, in Section 08.)

Seven projects, one future

As the company grew, my slate grew. At one point it held seven projects. Five were documentaries about people whose voices are rarely heard, each with a different topic, region and story. Two were narrative features I joined because I trusted the writers.

Judged one by one, every project made sense. Then, during my Oxford studies in change leadership, I looked at the slate as a whole, and I saw a problem I had missed (Rule 34).

The five documentaries were really one bet. They were all funded and bought by the same small group of impact funders and buyers, people who pay for films with a social purpose. All five depended on that group still wanting this kind of film three to five years later, when the films would be finished. If that demand shrank, it would shrink for all five at once.

My slate looked varied. It varied in topic, but not in risk. That is the trap in Rule 34: variety on the surface can hide one shared bet underneath.

The script in my own drawer

The fix was not to choose a “safer” project. It was to add a different one.

I had written 12 feature scripts myself. None of them was on my slate. I had only looked at projects I could reach through other people. My own scripts were the only projects I already fully owned.

I moved one of them, a horror film, onto the slate. Low-budget horror is financed and sold by different investors, buyers and distributors than impact documentaries. So it would fail under different conditions. That is what spreading a bet really means.

The early progress was fast. On a very small budget, I attached an award-winning screenwriter to rewrite the script and attached several of the key actors without paying them anything up front. Together, those actors reach millions of people on social media. That happened within weeks, in an industry where development usually takes years.

That is encouraging. It proves nothing yet. A fast start is a good signal, not a result.

Four futures

My first argument for the horror film was simple: horror had been growing for ten years, so it would keep growing. I even wrote that past success does not guarantee future success, and then said I was confident anyway.

That was a forecast pretending to be a fact. Nobody can know the future as a fact (Rule 35). So I ran my slate through a scenario exercise from my Oxford coursework. I chose the two forces that mattered most and were least certain for my business. I drew them as two crossing lines, which made four boxes, a standard scenario-planning method (Schwartz, 1991), and I wrote a short story of the future for each box. Then I tested the slate in each one.

The question changed from “Will horror stay popular?” to “Which of my projects survive in each of these futures, and which future would sink most of them at once?” That is a much more useful question, because it does not depend on guessing right.

Pause and guess: Pick a business in your city. Which two forces could change its world most in the next five years? Draw them as two crossing lines and describe the four futures.

Thirty bets

Today my slate has about 30 projects at different stages. It changes all the time. I add projects, pitch them, and drop the ones that do not pass the tests.

Many of my projects exist first as pitch decks. A deck is a cheap test. I package an idea, show it to investors, talent, crew and buyers, and see who leans in. If no one does, I learn that quickly and move on. That is failing fast on purpose.

Each project is run like its own company. It raises its own money, runs its own budget and carries out its own plan. Some are raising money now. Others are only ideas on paper.

That structure is itself a risk decision. No single project can sink the others, as long as I keep checking that they do not all depend on the same money, the same buyers or the same future.

What I would tell a student in your city

Make your first bet small enough to survive (Rule 37). Before you add a second bet, ask whether it would fail for the same reason as the first (Rule 34). Test your plan in more than one future (Rule 35). And when people tell you your bet looks foolish, ask yourself one question: is it improving fast (Rule 39)?
