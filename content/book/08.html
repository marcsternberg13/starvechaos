Primer

This is the first of five applied sections. The first seven sections were about how entrepreneurs think. These five are about what they do every week: sell, count money, lead people, deliver and build products.

Selling comes first because nothing else matters until someone buys. A sale is earned when you understand the buyer well enough that saying yes becomes easy for them. That means showing instead of describing, speaking to the buyer’s worries instead of your product’s features, mapping everyone who can say no, and putting your offer where buyers already are.

Marketing and sales are often confused. In this book, marketing is everything that makes the right buyer aware of you and ready to listen. Selling is the conversation that turns that readiness into a yes.

The main story is how I turned my early Amazon selling into a wholesale business: I sold the product first, then asked the brand for permission.

Key terms

  • Buyer. The person or organization that pays. The buyer is not always the user.
  • Decision-maker. The person who can sign. Often not the person who likes your idea most.
  • Gatekeeper. Someone who controls what information or offers reach the decision-maker.
  • Wholesale license. Permission to buy directly from a maker at a lower price, to resell.
  • Channel. The path a product takes to the customer: your own shop, a market stall, a website, a distributor or a marketplace.
  • Proof. Evidence a buyer accepts: sales already made, a working product, a test result.

The Rules

66 · Let people see the work before you ask Curiosity

People say yes more easily to something they can see and touch than to something you only describe. Show first, ask second. Show something finished enough to be judged fairly.

  • Boundary object: something people with different interests can look at together, such as a prototype, which helps them agree (Carlile, 2002).

→ See Sell first, ask second.

67 · Sell to their worry, not to your product Curiosity · Hunger

Buyers move when you solve what they value and what they fear. If you push your favorite product before you understand them, you will fail. Name their worry honestly, and promise only what you can keep.

68 · Your weakest claim decides what buyers believe Hunger

One weak claim, such as one you cannot prove, lowers how buyers judge everything else you say. People average what they hear; they do not add it up (Weaver et al., 2012). Say only what you can document.

69 · Find everyone who can say no, not just the buyer Curiosity

The person who loves your idea is rarely the person who signs, pays or can block it. Map all of them before you ask: users, influencers, buyers, deciders and gatekeepers (Webster & Wind, 1972).

→ See Everyone who could say no.

70 · A great pitch fails if the buyer cannot buy Curiosity · Chaos

Whether a sale closes depends on the buyer’s budget, buying process and decision-maker, not only on how good your message is. Check both: is your offer strong, and can this buyer act on it now?

  • Three questions: Is there money set aside? What steps must a purchase pass? Who can actually sign? Sales teams use a similar checklist called BANT (budget, authority, need, timing). On how organizations buy, see Webster and Wind (1972).

→ See When the supplier could not supply.

71 · Make the first yes small and easy Hunger · Chaos

A huge request forces a huge decision. Break it into smaller steps, and give buyers more than one way to agree. Make sure each small yes has a clear path to a bigger one.

  • Foot in the door: people who agree to a small request are more likely to agree to a bigger one later (Freedman & Fraser, 1966).

72 · Give each listener one clear ask Hunger

A document that asks many people for many things gets nothing done. Each audience gets its own short message and one request. Keep the full detail ready for anyone who wants it.

73 · When buyers don’t know they need it, teach first Curiosity

In a new market, people cannot want what they do not understand. Free teaching builds trust, and trust builds demand. Teach in a way that ties the trust to your name.

→ See Teaching brands about the digital shelf.

74 · Sell where your buyers already are Hunger · Curiosity

Do not wait for buyers to find you. Put your offer inside the moment and the place where they already buy.

  • Owner’s risk: selling inside someone else’s place means the owner can learn from your sales and compete with you (Zhu & Liu, 2018).

→ See Sell first, ask second.

The Story: Sell First, Ask Second

Sell first, ask second

In Section 02, I told how I started selling on Amazon in 2003, as a customer who saw empty shelves in the new categories. This is the selling side of that story: how a one-person listing habit became a wholesale business that introduced many brands to Amazon.

My method had three steps.

  1. Find products that were selling well in stores but were not yet listed on Amazon.
  2. Buy them and list them myself.
  3. Contact the brand directly and ask for a wholesale license.

The order matters. I did not go to a brand with a plan and ask for permission to try. I went to the brand after the product was already selling online. By the time I called, I could show them real orders from real customers on a channel they were not using yet (Rule 66).

I was also selling where buyers already were (Rule 74). Shoppers were already on Amazon, searching. I did not have to build a store or attract traffic. I put products in front of people at the moment they were looking.

Pause and guess: You call a brand owner and say you are already selling their product online. What do you think they say first?

Everyone who could say no

Getting a wholesale license was not a single yes from a single person. Before I could sell a brand’s products for the long term, I had to think about everyone who could stop it (Rule 69).

The brand’s owner or sales manager had to agree. But many brands already sold through stores and distributors, companies that buy from makers and resell to stores. A brand might worry about upsetting those partners. Amazon itself had rules about what could be listed and how. And the shoppers had to keep buying, or none of it mattered.

I learned to ask early: who here makes the decision, who else has to be comfortable, and what would make each of them say no?

Teaching brands about the digital shelf

In the early years, many brands did not understand what Amazon could do for them. Online selling was new. Some thought of Amazon as a bookstore. Others did not see why they should sell anywhere other than in stores.

So a large part of selling was teaching (Rule 73). I explained what the digital shelf was, how customers searched, how rankings worked and why being early mattered. I introduced many brands to Amazon this way, including fashion, jewelry and wearable brands, and helped them build their own storefronts on the site.

The teaching built trust before any money changed hands. And because I was the one teaching, the trust stayed with me.

Pause and guess: What would a store-based brand fear most about selling online?

The candle maker

My biggest success was in candles. I became friends with the owner of the company that made them. That relationship mattered more than any single order.

Together, we got the products ranked very high on the site. The candles sold so well that Amazon started buying from the company directly. I had set the brand up and made money doing it. Then Amazon cut me out and went straight to the maker, which also owned the brand. That was fine. What I had learned about selling candles stayed with me, and I went on to work with several other candle companies and many candle brands.

This is a sale I could not have closed with a pitch alone. Amazon is a very large buyer with its own process and its own decision-makers (Rule 70). What opened the door was proof: products that were already ranking, already selling, and a maker who trusted me.

When the supplier could not supply

My other big success was a laundry detergent. It sold very well. Then the detergent company moved to another state and shifted toward research and heavy commercial work. It stopped making the home products I had been selling.

Nothing about my selling had changed. The customers still wanted the product. But a sale needs both sides to be able to act, and the maker no longer could. The best pitch in the world cannot sell a product that is no longer made (Rule 70 works in both directions: the buyer must be able to buy, and the supplier must be able to supply).

That loss had a surprising result. Through that same detergent company, I learned about commercial laundry, and that led me to my laundry business (Sections 01 and 05).

What selling taught me

From 2003, and from 2005 inside the company I formed for this work, until 2010, the same lessons kept repeating.

Show, then ask. A brand that can see its products already selling says yes far more easily than one that is asked to imagine it. (Rule 66.)

Go where the buyers are. I never had to build traffic. I put products where people were already searching. (Rule 74.)

Teach when the market is new. When buyers do not yet understand the channel, the first sale is a lesson. (Rule 73.)

Map everyone, and check that both sides can act. Most lost sales are not lost in the pitch. They are lost because someone you did not see said no, or because one side could not act. (Rules 69 and 70.)

In your city, you can use the same method tomorrow. Find a product that already sells well in one bazaar, shop or city, but is not offered where its buyers also look, such as online, in another district or by delivery. Sell a small amount there first. Then go to the maker with your sales in your hand.
