Primer

Every business has more good ideas than time, money or people. So the hardest skill in building is not having ideas. It is choosing which ones not to pursue (Porter, 1996).

Focus is where hunger can hurt you. A hungry founder wants every customer, every market and every opportunity at once. That is how small teams spread thin and win nothing. Focus is how you turn hunger into results: you pick a few things to do excellently, write down what you will not do, and protect the few things customers value most.

The seven rules in this section are about saying no on purpose. The main story is how I cut the plan for one of my documentaries, Trail with No End, down to seven states, even when that meant giving up interviews I badly wanted.

Key terms

  • Focus. Putting limited time, money and attention on a few chosen things.
  • Priority. The thing that comes first when you cannot do everything.
  • “Will not do” list. A written list of things the business chooses not to do, so nobody adds them back one at a time.
  • Scope. The edges of a project: what is inside and what is outside.
  • Straddling. Trying to compete in two different ways at once, such as cheap and full-service, and doing neither well (Porter, 1996).
  • Parking. Setting an idea aside with a date to look at it again, instead of throwing it away.

The Rules

59 · Your strategy is mostly what you refuse to do Chaos · Hunger

If you try to be cheap and full-service at the same time, you end up good at neither (Porter, 1996). Real strategy picks a few things to do well, then writes down what you will not do, so nobody adds them back.

  • Review on purpose: the list is for now, not forever. When customers or costs really change, review the whole list. Do not let exceptions creep in one at a time.

→ See Drawing the map first.

60 · Choose your customer, then say no to the rest Hunger

When you know exactly who you serve, you can refuse business that looks attractive but would pull you away from them. Check that the group you choose is large enough to pay your bills.

61 · Leaving yesterday’s business is a decision, not defeat Chaos · Curiosity

The old business feels like home because you built it. Ask what a new owner, with no memories tied to it, would stop doing (Grove, 1996). Then stop it yourself.

62 · Bigger is not better when profit gets smaller Hunger

Adding products and markets can raise revenue (all the money that comes in) while it quietly weakens the business that made you strong. Watch profit, not size. Growth into related areas, using the strengths you already have, tends to work best (Palich et al., 2000).

63 · Win one place completely before you try five Chaos · Hunger

A small team spread across many markets wins none of them. Narrow down to the one place you can win, and park the rest with a date to look again.

  • Attention is the scarce resource: what a firm does depends on where its managers point their limited attention (Ocasio, 1997).

→ See Seven states.

64 · Listen to everyone, then let one person decide Chaos

Waiting for everyone to agree kills speed and hides who is responsible. Gather views widely. Then one owner decides, explains why, and answers for the result.

  • Decisions over options: when you bring a problem to the decider, bring a recommended decision with a short reason, not a menu.
  • Compare: the RAPID approach gives each decision one clear owner, “the D” (Rogers & Blenko, 2006).

65 · Decide first what you will never cut Hunger · Curiosity

Before you cut anything, name the two or three things customers feel most and protect them. Everything else can bend. If everything is “never cut,” nothing is protected.

→ See What we would never cut.

The Story: Seven States

The project

Trail with No End is the working title of a feature documentary I am producing about Poteet Victory, a Cherokee and Choctaw painter born in Idabel, Oklahoma, who now owns his own gallery in Santa Fe, New Mexico. His work is in the permanent collection of the Smithsonian Institution. The film is about Poteet, his art and the Trail of Tears, the forced removal of Native nations in the 1830s, seen in a modern context. It is told through his paintings and through the people who know him and collect his work.

At the center of the film is a piece of work that has never been seen. In 2000, a university museum commissioned Poteet to paint a monumental mural of the Trail of Tears. He finished two panels. Then the project was abruptly canceled. The reasons were complex: the timing, the project itself and the funding all played a part. The mural has been rolled up in storage ever since and has never been exhibited.

This is not a film about blame. I am not interested in proving who was right in 2000. I am interested in the man, the work and what the work means now. That choice shaped every other decision on the project.

Like every film on my slate, this one runs as its own small company. It has its own budget, its own investors and its own plan. Nothing about it is finished yet. That is exactly why it is a good story about focus: every choice below was made with too little money, too little time and too many good options.

Too many good people

A documentary lives or dies on its interviews. Early on, my team built a list of the 30 most important people to interview: artists, collectors, curators, museum leaders, historians and people who had known Poteet for decades.

Every name on that list was a good one. That was the problem. Those 30 people lived all over the United States. Interviewing all of them meant travel we could not afford, a schedule we could not keep and a small team stretched across the whole country.

This is the trap in Rule 63. When every option is good, a small team tries to do all of them, and does none of them well. Good options are more dangerous than bad ones, because nobody argues against them.

Drawing the map first

So before we decided who to interview, we decided where we would film. I limited the shoot to seven states.

That one decision did more work than any ranking could. A long argument about 30 people became a simple question for each name: does this person live inside the map or outside it?

Seven people on our original list lived outside those seven states. They were replaced. One of them was the person my team called “the most important interview in the project.” I did not let that one hard case break the whole plan. I took it out of the general list and made it a separate decision, to be judged on its own, later.

That was Rule 59 in practice: a strategy is mostly what you refuse to do. The seven-state map was a written “will not do.” We will not chase interviews across the whole country, even good ones.

Pause and guess: If you could work in only seven places, how would you choose them?

Grouping by trip, not by importance

Next, we took the people inside the map and grouped them by location instead of by importance. The ranked list told us who mattered most. The travel list told us what each trip could actually cover.

Thirty contacts became nine travel groups. Three of those groups covered 16 of the 30 people. That told us where to spend our first and biggest trips, and which trips could wait.

Later, I dropped a planned trip to southeastern Oklahoma because of the distance. It was a real loss. It was also the right call. One long trip would have used the time and money of several shorter, fuller ones.

What we would never cut

Focus is not only about cutting. It is also about deciding, before the cutting starts, what is protected (Rule 65). On this film, two things were never on the table.

The first was the center of the film. The film is about Poteet’s life and art, not only about the mural. At one point I cut a whole page from our pitch materials that laid out a three-act structure, because it made the film look like it was only about the mural. Some of the useful parts moved elsewhere. The page itself went. A good page that moves the center of the product is not a good page.

The second was the separation of money. The film has a budget. Separately, there is a fund to help Poteet finish the mural. The two never appear in the same document. In our investor materials, the film budget “ring-fences” the mural fund, which means that money is kept apart and cannot be spent on the film. Mixing two pools of money confuses everyone about what they are paying for.

Everything else could bend: which interviews, which trips, which days. Those two could not.

Four audiences, four documents

The same discipline applies to how we ask for help. My team writes to four groups: investors, donors, institutions and the people we hope to interview. Each group gets its own document, with its own single request. No document carries another group’s ask.

An early version of our investor deck ran 45 pages. When we reviewed it, about 44% of it turned out to be internal research, such as contact lists and interview plans. Investors do not need that. We rebuilt it as a 20-page investor document: the story, the budget, the team, the schedule, how the film will reach audiences and the ask. (Giving each listener one clear ask is Rule 72, in Section 08.)

Decisions over options

One working rule holds all of this together. When someone on my team brings me a problem, I expect a recommended decision with a short reason, not a menu of choices. A menu is fine when a person is truly stuck. Otherwise, the person closest to the problem recommends, and I decide and answer for the result (Rule 64).

That rule keeps a small team moving. Seven states, nine trips, four documents and two things we would never cut: none of those choices came from waiting until everyone agreed.

Your turn to draw a map

Pick a small business idea in your city, such as a delivery service or a tutoring center. Before you list customers, draw your map: which neighborhoods, which streets, which hours? Then write down the best customer you are giving up by drawing that map, and park it with a date. If that feels painful, you are probably doing it right.
