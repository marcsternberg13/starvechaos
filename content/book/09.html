Primer

A business lives or dies on a few numbers. You do not need to be an accountant to know them, but you do need to find them, read them and act on them.

The most important money idea in this section is simple: profit is the gap between what customers pay you and what it costs you to serve them. Everything else follows from that. How you set the price. How you count every cost between the price and your pocket. How you pay people. How many sources of money you depend on. How long your cash will last.

Money is also where chaos hides best. A business can look busy and successful and still be losing money on every sale. Starving chaos, in finance, means knowing your numbers well enough that nothing surprises you twice.

The main story is how I priced my teenage landscaping jobs, and how changing the way I charged, not the amount, changed everything.

Key terms

  • Revenue. All the money that comes in from sales, before any costs.
  • Cost of goods. What the product itself costs to make or buy.
  • Gross margin. The share of the price left after the cost of goods. A $10 sale of a $4 product has a 60% gross margin.
  • Net margin. The share of the price left after all costs: ads, shipping, returns, fees, rent and wages.
  • Runway. How many months your cash will last at your current spending.
  • Contingency. Extra money set aside for surprises.
  • Price model. How you charge: per hour, per project, by subscription, per result, or with someone else paying.

The Rules

75 · Count every cost between the price and your pocket Hunger · Curiosity

A product can look very profitable on paper and still lose money. Many costs sit between the price a customer pays and the money you keep: ads, returns, shipping and fees to the channel that sells for you. Follow one sale all the way to the end.

  • Gross vs. net: gross margin shows only the product cost. Net margin shows what you keep.
  • Customer value: a first sale that loses money can still pay off if the customer comes back. If they buy once and leave, it is a loss (Gupta et al., 2004).

→ See The worksheet.

76 · Profit lives in the gap between price and cost Curiosity · Chaos

Profit is the gap between what customers pay and what it costs to serve them. You can make your gap wider than rivals’ in two ways: customers pay you more because they value you more, or your costs are lower (Porter, 1996). Know which side you win on.

→ See Two sides of the gap.

77 · Pay for results, not for promises Hunger · Chaos

Fixed costs are dangerous when cash is short. You can turn some of them into shared upside: lower pay now and a share of profit later, so cash goes out when value comes in. Write exactly what the share is calculated on, or good people will say no.

  • Net vs. gross: “net” means money after costs; “gross” means money before costs. A promise of a share must say which.

78 · Count the people your money depends on Chaos

List everyone who pays your bills: customers, funders and the channels that sell for you. If one source pays most of the bills, one decision by that source can end you (Froelich, 1999).

79 · Assume the money will run out sooner Chaos · Hunger

Costs rise and cash arrives late. Count your runway, price the whole path to your goal, and keep a contingency. In one study of 258 large transport projects, costs were underestimated in about 9 out of 10, by 28% on average (Flyvbjerg et al., 2002).

80 · Choose the number you agree to be judged by Curiosity

What you measure decides what people chase. Pick numbers that show the real health of the business, not the ones that grow fastest or look best. Use two or three numbers that check each other (Grove, 1983).

  • Rewarding A, hoping for B: organizations get the behavior they reward, not the behavior they hope for (Kerr, 1975).

81 · Change how you charge, not only how much Curiosity · Hunger

The price model can matter more than the price number. You can charge per hour, per project, per use, by subscription, or arrange for someone else to pay. Each model changes who buys and how often.

  • Taximeter effect: paying per use feels like watching a taxi meter run, so many customers prefer one fixed price (Lambrecht & Skiera, 2006).

→ See Quote the job, not the hour.

82 · A price needs proof the buyer can believe Curiosity

Your price must rest on evidence the buyer accepts: a test, an independent appraisal, or a price someone already paid. Your hope is not evidence. Buyers also read the price itself as a signal of quality (Rao & Monroe, 1989).

The Story: Quote the Job, Not the Hour

Laughed at

When I was 16 and running my landscaping business, I priced the way I thought prices worked: by the hour. I told customers an hourly rate. It depended on the job, but think of something like $100 an hour.

They laughed in my face.

I was young. To an adult homeowner, a high hourly rate from a teenager sounded ridiculous. They pictured a kid slowly raking leaves while the meter ran. The number I said out loud was being compared with what they thought my time was worth, not with the value of a clean yard.

Pause and guess: What would you say to a 16-year-old who asked you for $100 an hour to clean your yard? Now, what if he offered one price for the whole job instead?

Quote the job, not the hour

So I changed how I charged (Rule 81). Instead of an hourly rate, I gave one flat price for the whole project: this yard, cleared, for this amount.

Customers accepted it.

The work was the same. The yard was the same. What changed was what the customer was thinking about. With an hourly rate, they thought about my time and worried how many hours I would take. With a project price, they thought about the result: a finished yard, and a number they knew in advance. There was no meter running.

Working backward to $300 an hour

After each job, I did something important. I took the project price and divided it by the hours I had actually worked. That told me my real hourly rate.

On some jobs, it came to as much as $300 an hour. I was a teenager.

If I had quoted $300 an hour, nobody would have hired me. Quoting the job, I earned it, and the customers were happy, because they were paying for a result they valued at a price they had agreed to.

Two sides of the gap

How could a teenager earn that much per hour? Rule 76 explains it. Profit is the gap between price and cost, and I was widening the gap from both sides at once.

On the price side, the project price was set by what a finished yard was worth to the customer, not by what an hour of a teenager’s time sounded like.

On the cost side, my equipment did the work very fast. As I told in Section 03, I had invested in fall and winter tools that none of my competitors had. A job that would take someone else much longer took me a fraction of the time. Fewer hours per job meant a much lower cost per job, for the same price.

A high price and a low cost: that is a wide gap. That is where the $300 came from.

What was inside the price

The $300 an hour was not all profit. Between the price and my pocket sat real costs: the equipment, which I had to pay off, fuel, maintenance, and the time I spent traveling between yards and getting the work (Rule 75).

Pause and guess: List every cost hidden inside the price of one landscaping job.

As a teenager, I learned to look at what a job really earned only after all of that was counted. Later in my career, I turned that habit into a tool.

The worksheet

In my consulting work with manufacturers, I build a margin worksheet for a product before a client decides where to sell it. It follows one sale from the price all the way down to what the client keeps, one cost at a time.

Here is one product as an example. It cost $5 to make. Sold to shops at $10.64, it had a 53% gross margin. Sold directly to customers at $20, it had a 75% gross margin. On paper, both looked healthy.

Then the worksheet took away the other costs, one line at a time: ads, returns, shipping, warehouse fees and channel fees. Each was a share of the price.

Channel Gross margin What was left at the bottom (net margin)
Selling to shops 53% about 32%
The client’s own web store 75% about 15%
A big-box online marketplace 75% about −6%

The channel with the best-looking gross margin was the worst in the end. On the big marketplace, heavy shipping and warehouse fees meant the client would lose money on every sale. On the client’s own web store, one change helped a lot: when customers bought two items in one order instead of one, net margin rose from about 15% to about 24%, because shipping and handling were shared across both items.

This was one product, not a rule. Gross margins on big marketplaces vary widely from product to product; in my work they have ranged from about 10% to 75%. That is exactly why the worksheet has to be done one product at a time.

That is the same lesson as the landscaping jobs, written in a spreadsheet. The number that sounds good at the top is not the number you keep.

What I would tell a seller at the bazaar

First, try charging for the result instead of the time. A tailor can quote the finished dress, not the hours. A tutor can quote a package of lessons that prepares a student for one exam. (Rule 81.)

Second, after each job, divide what you earned by the hours you worked. Know your real rate.

Third, follow one sale all the way to your pocket. Write the price at the top. Subtract every cost: materials, transport, rent, the shop’s commission, broken items. The last number is the one that matters. (Rule 75.)

And fourth, ask which side of the gap you win on. Do customers pay you more because they value you more, or do you serve them for less? If the answer is “neither,” that is your first problem to solve. (Rule 76.)
