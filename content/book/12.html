Primer

The product is where everything in this book meets a customer: your hunger to build, your curiosity about what people need, and the chaos of the real world, where people use things in ways you never planned.

Building a product is a series of choices. What is it for? What must it do perfectly, and what can be rough? What should be cut? Can you afford to make it, and keep making it? Does your brand promise that it will stay the same, or that it will keep changing? The six rules in this section help you make those choices on purpose.

The main story returns to my fashion-technology startup from Section 06. There, the story was about losing a company. Here, it is about the product itself: what it was, what it was for, and what I would decide differently before building it today.

Key terms

  • Product. What the customer gets: a physical item, a service, software or a mix.
  • Core. The one thing the product must do well for anyone to want it.
  • Feature. One thing a product can do. More features are not always better.
  • Prototype. An early, rough working version used to learn.
  • Patent. A legal right that stops others from copying an invention for a period of time. It protects an idea; it does not build or sell it.
  • Brand promise. What customers expect from you every time, such as “always the same” or “always something new.”

The Rules

Rule 46 · A test only counts where the customer really is — see Section 05. For a maker: before you finish a product, put a rough version into a real customer’s hands, in their place, and watch what breaks outside the thing you built.

96 · Some products must not ship until they are right Curiosity · Hunger

Speed matters, but some products get only one first impression. Know which kind you are making. If it is a one-impression product, hold it back while the core is not working. If customers will accept a rough first version, release it and improve it.

→ See What it had to get right.

97 · Remove features until only the essential is left Curiosity · Hunger

Strong products are excellent at a few things, not at everything. Cut, reduce and raise on purpose, so that what remains stands out (Kim & Mauborgne, 2005). Cut what customers do not value, not just what is hard for you to make.

  • Feature fatigue: buyers often choose the product with more features, then like it less after using it, because the extra features make it harder to use (Thompson et al., 2005).

98 · Users will show you what your product really is Curiosity

Launch is where discovery starts. Watch how people actually use your product, because a small side feature may turn out to be the main business. Before you change the product, check that many customers share the new use and will pay for it.

→ See What the retailers saw.

99 · Design the product around money you can sustain Hunger

Shape the product to fit what you can afford to make and supply, so each sale helps pay for the next. Decide by affordable loss: design it so your business survives even if it sells less than you hope (Sarasvathy, 2001).

→ See A product you cannot afford to finish.

100 · Agree what the product is for before building Curiosity

Teams waste years when each person imagines a different product. Before you build, agree on the change the product should create for the person who uses it: the same person, before and after (Checkland, 1981).

  • Worldview: different people can see different purposes in the same product. Make those views visible and agree on one before you build (Checkland, 1981).

→ See Three products in one.

101 · Know if your brand promises sameness or change Curiosity

Some products are loved because they never change. Others are loved because they keep improving. Know which promise your brand has made before you change the product. Even a “sameness” brand must improve quality, safety and service.

The Story: A Digital Wardrobe, Twenty Years Early

The idea

During my freshman year at Arizona State University, while I was working part-time as a salesperson in a high-end fashion store, I came up with the idea for a digital wardrobe. It would be software that a customer paid for by subscription, together with a service built around it. Later, after I had built a commercial laundry company, I also saw a way to connect the two.

The concept received two patents. I founded a startup to build it, moved the company from Scottsdale, Arizona, to San Francisco, built a prototype and tested the idea with retailers. In Section 06, I told how the company ran out of money because I spent my capital on patents instead of software. Here I want to look at the product itself.

Pause and guess: What would you want a digital wardrobe to do for you on a normal morning?

Three products in one

Looking back, the digital wardrobe could serve at least three different people, and each one would have wanted a different product (Rule 100).

  • The shopper would want a tool to see, organize and use the clothes they already owned.
  • The retailer would want a tool to sell more, by knowing what customers already had and what they might need next.
  • The laundry would want a tool that tracked garments through cleaning and care.

Each of those is a real product. Each has a different buyer, a different price and a different first version. A product that tries to be all three at once is very hard to build with little money, and very hard to explain to an investor in a short meeting.

Pause and guess: Which of these three customers would you build for first, and why?

If I were starting today, I would write one sentence before building anything: this product turns a [specific person] in [one condition] into the same person in [a better condition] (Checkland, 1981). Then I would build only the version that makes that one change.

What the retailers saw

I tested the idea with retailers. That was the right instinct: put the idea in front of real customers early (Rule 46, and Rule 98).

Whatever they said, the lesson of Rule 98 is to listen for the use you did not design. Sometimes a side feature that customers keep asking about is the real business. Sometimes it is just one unusual customer. The test is whether many customers want it and will pay.

What it had to get right

Some products can launch rough and improve. Others get only one first impression (Rule 96). A digital wardrobe is close to the second kind. If a customer spends an evening entering their clothes and the product loses them, shows them wrong, or gives useless suggestions, that customer does not come back.

So the core of this product was not the patent. It was the experience of getting your wardrobe into the product easily and getting something useful back right away. That core had to work before launch.

A product you cannot afford to finish

The hardest lesson is the one in Rule 99. I designed a product that needed a great deal of money to build and to keep running: software, data, retail partners and possibly a laundry service. I did not have that money, and I spent much of what I had on legal protection.

A product designed around money you can sustain would have looked different. It might have been one small, working piece: a version for one kind of customer, built cheaply, earning a little money that paid for the next piece. That smaller product would have been less impressive in a pitch meeting. It might still be alive.

Twenty years later

Twenty years later, the product I imagined still has not been built. That tells me the idea was early, not simply wrong. But early ideas need either patience you can pay for, or a first product small enough to survive while the world catches up.

Before you build, in your city

Before you build anything, a new snack, an app, a tailoring service, answer four questions on one page.

  1. Who exactly is it for, and what change will it make for them? Write one sentence. (Rule 100.)
  2. Is it a one-impression product, or can it launch rough and improve? (Rule 96.)
  3. What is the smallest version you can afford to make and keep making? (Rule 99.)
  4. Who will you show it to first, in their own place, before it is finished? (Rules 46 and 98.)
