Primer

You will almost never have enough money, people or time. That is not a sign that you started wrong. It is the normal condition of a new business. Resourcefulness is the skill of building something real from what you have now, instead of waiting for what you wish you had.

Resourceful founders do three things well. They choose where to spend, and spend heavily there. They look hard at what they already own before they buy. And they treat a missing resource as a design problem, not a reason to stop. The twelve rules in this section show how.

The main story is my teenage landscaping business, which began with cheap tools and grew because I put my money into equipment my competitors did not have.

Key terms

  • Resources. Everything a business can use: money, time, skills, tools, space, materials and the people you know.
  • Bootstrapping. Building a business with your own money and early sales, without outside investors.
  • Bricolage. Making do with the resources at hand, combining them in new ways (Baker & Nelson, 2005).
  • Affordable loss. The amount you can lose without serious harm. Experienced founders often decide how much to risk this way, instead of predicting how much they will earn (Sarasvathy, 2001).
  • Fixed cost. A cost you pay whether you sell or not, such as rent.
  • Variable cost. A cost that rises with each sale, such as materials.

The Rules

22 · Spend big on one thing, cheap on everything else Hunger · Chaos

A small budget forces a choice. Put the money where the customer feels it most, and keep everything else simple. Money spread evenly across everything buys nothing that stands out.

→ See The one thing worth real money.

23 · Build the ugly version first; it only has to work Chaos · Hunger

When the right tool costs too much, build a rough one from what is at hand. It does not need to look good. It needs to work well enough to teach you something. Improve it once it proves useful.

24 · Cut every cost the customer never sees Hunger

Save on what buyers never see and never on what they value. Test every cut with one question: will the customer notice?

  • Hidden is not always hidden: cutting safety, staff pay or quality checks saves money today and costs trust later.

→ See Cheap tools first.

25 · Look at what you already own before you buy Curiosity · Chaos

Most ventures sit on unused assets: space, machines, skills, materials and relationships. Using them for something new is often faster and cheaper than buying anything new. Before you spend, list what you already have.

26 · Own what makes you special; borrow the rest Hunger · Curiosity

Keep the one thing nobody can copy. Rent, partner or borrow everything else. This keeps costs flexible and lets a small firm move faster than if it built everything itself.

27 · Let a small business pay for the big dream Hunger

Use a skill you already have to earn money now, and let that income pay for the goal you really want. A small working business can buy you time and freedom that a loan cannot.

  • Hybrid entry: starting a business while keeping a job. People who did this had a lower risk of closing their business than those who quit first (Raffiee & Feng, 2014).

28 · In a cash crisis, ask the people doing the work Chaos · Hunger

The best ideas for saving money often sit with frontline staff, the people who do the daily work. Asking them is free, and their ideas often beat cuts decided at the top. Share the real numbers, and act on good ideas, or people stop answering.

29 · No money? Change the shape of the deal Curiosity · Hunger

When you cannot pay more or outbid others, redesign the deal. Give the other side something they value other than cash: a long relationship, a share of the savings, or a new way to pay.

30 · Start with the money you have, not the money you want Hunger · Curiosity

Start with what you already have: savings, skills and the people you know. Risk only what you can afford to lose (Sarasvathy, 2001). Waiting for the money you want can mean you never start.

→ See Before landscaping.

31 · Do things by hand before you build the machine Hunger · Curiosity

Early on, do the work yourself, one customer at a time. Paul Graham (2013) calls this doing things that don’t scale. You learn what matters before you pay to automate it. Work by hand is slow, but it tells you what to build.

→ See Cheap tools first.

32 · Go where the big players won’t Hunger · Chaos

Large firms often skip small places, small customers and awkward seasons. Those markets are often cheaper to serve and easier to win. Start where the big firms are not looking, and check that the customers there can still pay you.

33 · Turn a weakness into an advantage Chaos · Curiosity

Lacking something your rivals have can push you to a better design. List what you lack. Then ask how that gap could help you. Limits help creativity up to a point; too many limits, or none, can hurt (Acar et al., 2019).

→ See What a teenager lacks.

The Story: The Right Machine for the Leaves

Before landscaping

My first business was a lemonade stand at my parents’ yard sale, when I was eight (see Part 1). It needed almost no money. I used what was already in the house and sold to the people who were already there (Rule 30).

After that, my neighbors started asking me for help. They needed someone to babysit, to watch their dog, to look after their house while they traveled, or to walk the dog every day. I did not invent those services. I supplied what people already needed and asked for.

Those small jobs taught me the first lesson of resourcefulness: you can start with almost nothing if you begin with a real need and the resources you already have. The money from those jobs, and the trust of those neighbors, were the resources I took into landscaping.

Cheap tools first

When I started landscaping, I bought cheap equipment. It was what I could afford. It did the job, slowly, and that was enough to begin.

This is Rule 31 in its simplest form. I did the work myself, by hand and with basic tools, one yard at a time. Doing it myself showed me which jobs took the longest, which jobs customers cared about most, and which tasks a better machine would change completely. I did not know those things on the first day. I learned them in the yards.

The cheap tools also kept my costs low in a place customers did not look (Rule 24). A customer does not care what brand of rake you carry. They care whether the yard is clean and the job is done when you said. So I saved on the tool and put my effort into the result.

As I saved money, I upgraded. Cheap equipment became good equipment, and good equipment became great equipment. Each upgrade was paid for by the work the last set of tools had done.

The season the others skipped

My business did not stop when summer ended. I worked in the fall and the winter too. Those seasons need special equipment. Fall brings huge volumes of leaves. Winter jobs need their own tools.

Many people think of landscaping as a summer business. The fall and winter work was harder, and it needed tools that cost real money. That is exactly why it was an opening.

The one thing worth real money

Here is the choice that made the business. I invested in fall and winter landscaping tools that none of my competitors had (Rule 22).

I did not spread my money evenly across everything. I did not buy a nicer truck or fancy uniforms or advertising. I put the money into the one thing that changed how fast I could do the hardest jobs of the year.

Those high-tech tools made the jobs very fast. A job that took a competitor most of a day took me far less. Because the work went fast, I could charge a strong price for the job and still earn a very high amount for every hour I worked. (How I priced those jobs is its own lesson. It is the story in Section 09.)

The equipment also paid for itself quickly. The volume of leaves in the fall was so large that the machines earned back their cost in a short time. After that, every job they did was mostly profit.

Pause and guess: How would you work out how long a new machine takes to pay for itself?

What a teenager lacks

As a teenager, I lacked almost everything a real landscaping company has: a crew, a reputation, years of experience and money.

Rule 33 says to list what you lack and ask how the gap could help. A teenager cannot hire a large crew. The right machine closes that gap: it lets one young person do the work of several, and the money that would have gone to wages stays in the business.

What I still do

I have used the same pattern in almost every business since, including in film, where budgets are always too small.

Start with what you have. Lemonade, yard sales and neighbors’ requests needed no investor. (Rule 30.)

Do the work yourself before you buy the machine. The cheap tools taught me where a better one would matter. (Rule 31.)

Save where the customer does not look. Nobody paid me for my rake. They paid me for a clean yard. (Rule 24.)

Then spend big on the one thing that changes the result. The fall and winter equipment did more for my business than everything else I owned put together. (Rule 22.)

Look at a business near you. Where is it spending a little on everything? And what is the one tool, skill or machine that, if it bought the best version, would change how fast or how well it serves its customers?
