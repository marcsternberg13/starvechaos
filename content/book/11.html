Primer

An idea is worth little until someone delivers it, on time, again and again. Operations is the daily work of making, shipping and finishing. Execution is doing what you said you would do.

This is where chaos is most visible. A late supplier, a wrong price on a website, a step that only one person knows how to do, a launch that tries to do everything at once: each of these turns a good plan into a bad week. Starving chaos in operations means finding the slowest step, keeping one trusted version of your information, turning repeated work into routines, growing in steps, and planning for the delay you cannot see yet.

The main story comes from my years building online sales inside established companies: a 100-product launch, a catalog of more than 11,000 products, and a year of “boring” data work that paid off in the year after. As with all my employer stories, the companies and people are not named.

Key terms

  • Operations. The repeated work that delivers the product or service to customers.
  • Bottleneck (constraint). The one step, part or supplier that limits how fast the whole operation can move (Goldratt & Cox, 1984).
  • Master data. The single trusted record of key facts, such as product names, prices and descriptions, from which every other copy is made.
  • Routine. A written, repeatable way of doing a task, so it does not depend on one person’s memory.
  • Pilot. A small trial in one place before a wider rollout.
  • Contingency. Spare time and money set aside before anything goes wrong.

The Rules

90 · The slowest part sets the speed of everything Chaos · Hunger

Every operation has one step, part or supplier that is slower or scarcer than the rest. The whole business can only move as fast as that piece. Find it first and fix it first. When you fix it, another step becomes the slowest, so look again (Goldratt & Cox, 1984).

→ See A hundred products at once.

91 · Keep one master version that everyone can trust Curiosity

When copies multiply, errors multiply. Keep one clean master of your important data and documents, and replace old versions instead of patching them, so every decision starts from the same facts. Make updating the master easy, or people will start making private copies again.

→ See The boring year.

92 · Turn the work you repeat into a system Hunger

You cannot do everything yourself forever. Turn each task you repeat into a written routine, so the business runs well even when you are not in the room. Keep routines for work that really repeats; one-time, special work needs judgment.

→ See Eleven thousand products.

93 · Change one piece and the other pieces must change Chaos · Curiosity

Tasks, people, structure and culture fit together like parts of one machine. When you change a tool or a product, redesign the team and the habits around it too (Trist & Bamforth, 1951). A small change may fit the old habits; a big one rarely does.

94 · Scale in steps: one site, then a few, then all Curiosity · Hunger

Prove the work in one small place, fix what breaks, then expand in order of importance. If you launch everywhere at once, every mistake spreads everywhere. Know why you are pausing, and set a date for the next step.

→ See A hundred products at once.

95 · Plan for the delay you cannot see yet Chaos

Big projects almost always take longer and cost more than planned (Flyvbjerg, 2014). Build spare days and money into the plan before anything goes wrong. Plan from how long similar projects really took, not from your own hopeful estimate.

  • Outside view: judge your project by the record of similar projects, not by its own plan (Flyvbjerg, 2014; Kahneman & Lovallo, 1993).

The Story: A Hundred Products, Eleven Thousand Products, One Master File

A hundred products at once

From 2014 to 2016, I was Ecommerce Director at a company that made digital products. I led a team of more than 30 people across three regions. During that time, we launched a new digital brand with 100 products at once. In its first month, it brought in more than $400,000 in revenue.

A launch like that is a lesson in Rule 90. With 100 products going live together, the launch can only move as fast as its slowest piece: one late file, one missing approval, one supplier, one page that will not load. A single weak link can hold up all 100 products.

Pause and guess: You are launching 100 products tomorrow. What is the one thing most likely to break?

It also raises a fair question about Rule 94. The rule says to scale in steps: prove one, then a few, then all. We launched 100 at once.

Pause and guess: Would you launch 100 products at once, or 10 first? Why?

Eleven thousand products

From 2020 to 2022, I was Vice President of Digital at a company that sold through many online storefronts. The business had more than 11,000 products across 10 online storefronts. I led an international team with 32 people reporting directly to me. Growth was 150% in one year, and then 100% the year after.

At that scale, heroes do not work. Nobody, including the VP, can personally check 11,000 products in 10 stores. With 32 direct reports, I could not even attend every important meeting. The only way to keep quality high was to turn every task that repeated into a routine that anyone on the team could follow (Rule 92).

The boring year

From 2022 to 2023, I was Director of Ecommerce at a specialty food brand. In the first year, online sales grew 23%. In the second year, they grew 140%.

The difference between those two years is the lesson. In the first year, I led the rollout of a product-information system. That is software that holds one master record for every product: its name, description, price, ingredients, images and everything else a store or website needs. Every other copy, on every website and in every marketplace listing, is made from that master (Rule 91).

That kind of work is not exciting. It does not look like growth. For a year, much of the effort went into cleaning and organizing information instead of launching new things. But once there was one trusted master, every new listing, every change and every new channel became faster and safer. The second year’s growth was built on the first year’s boring work.

Pause and guess: Why would a company spend a whole year on work its customers never see?

The same rule in film

I use the same master-file rule in my film company today. Every important document starts as one master. Every other format, such as a web page, a PDF or slides, is made from that master and never edited on its own. When I approve a version, it is frozen as a record, and every change after that becomes a new version number. Nobody has to wonder which file is the right one.

We also learned that a correction must be applied everywhere the error appears, not just in the one place someone noticed it. Facts that had been removed as wrong kept coming back in new drafts, until we started checking every new version for old errors.

What I would tell a business owner in your city

Walk through your business from the customer’s first question to the finished sale, and find the step where the line or the pile is longest. Fix that step first (Rule 90).

Write down your prices and product facts in one place, and update every other copy from it: the sign, the notebook, the phone messages (Rule 91).

Take one task you do every day, such as opening the shop or packing an order, and write it as five steps that a new helper could follow without asking you (Rule 92).

And when you are ready to grow, open one new location, route or product line first, learn from it, and only then open the next (Rule 94).
