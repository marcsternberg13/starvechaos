Some of the most dangerous advice in business is short, confident and repeated so often that nobody checks it. These ten phrases all contain some truth, which is why people keep saying them. Each one also leads founders into chaos when it is used without thinking.

Each phrase follows the same four steps: what it means, why people say it, how it gets overused or misread, and why it is dangerous. Each ends by pointing to the rules that answer it. Read one before class, or before a big decision.

Phrase 1 · “The customer is always right”

What it means. Treat every customer’s wish as correct, and do what they ask.

Why people say it. Because businesses that ignore customers die. The phrase pushes back against shops that treat buyers rudely, overcharge them or refuse to take back bad goods. It reminds staff that the customer pays everyone’s wages, and that a complaint is often free information about what is going wrong.

How it gets overused or misread. The phrase quietly turns into two other ideas. The first is “every customer is our customer,” so a business tries to please everyone and ends up excellent for no one. The second is “whatever customers say is true,” so a business builds what customers ask for in surveys and is surprised when they do not buy it. Staff also hear it as “never say no,” even when a customer is asking for something unfair, unsafe or impossible to deliver at the price.

Why it’s dangerous. First, not every customer is your customer. Choosing who you serve is one of the most important decisions in a business. When I built my laundry company, I chose high-end, environmentally and socially responsible businesses and built everything around them. A business that only wanted the cheapest wash was not our customer, however big it was. Saying no to that business is what let us charge three times our competitors’ price per pound to the customers we did choose. If we had treated every customer as right, we would have become one more cheap laundry.

Second, customers are often wrong about themselves. Not because they lie, but because people’s explanations for their own choices are usually stories, not records (Nisbett & Wilson, 1977). Coca-Cola’s new formula was preferred in taste tests of nearly 200,000 consumers. When the company replaced the old drink with “New Coke” on April 23, 1985, customers demanded the original back, and it returned on July 11, 1985, 79 days later (The Coca-Cola Company, n.d.). The customers were right about the taste of one sip. They were wrong about what they would do when a familiar drink disappeared.

The better version of the phrase is this: the customer’s behavior is always information. Listen hard to customers, especially when they complain. Watch what they actually do. Then decide, as the owner, which customers you will serve and how. When the students at my university were already paying $40 a bag for laundry, that was the truth about demand. No survey answer could have told me more.

Answered by: Rule 43 (believe what customers did, not the reasons they give) and Rule 60 (choose your customer, then say no to the rest).

Phrase 2 · “A great product sells itself”

What it means. If you make something excellent enough, buyers will find it and buy it without much effort from you.

Why people say it. Because quality really does matter, and many founders spend too much on marketing a weak product. Excellent products earn word of mouth, repeat customers and loyalty that advertising cannot buy. The phrase is also a comfort to makers who would rather build than sell.

How it gets overused or misread. It becomes an excuse not to sell. Founders keep improving the product and wait for customers to arrive. When they do not, the founder concludes the product is not good enough yet, and improves it again. Meanwhile, nobody who could buy it knows it exists, or the people who love it are not the people who can pay.

Why it’s dangerous. A sale needs more than a good product. Someone has to find the buyer. The buyer needs a budget, a process that allows the purchase and a person with the authority to say yes (Webster & Wind, 1972). And the maker has to be able to keep supplying it.

I saw every one of those break in my own work. My early Amazon business had a laundry detergent that sold very well. Customers loved it. Then the company that made it moved to another state, shifted to commercial work and stopped making the home product. A great product with happy buyers stopped selling overnight, because one side of the sale could no longer act.

My best Amazon success, in candles, did not sell itself either. It worked because I became friends with the owner of the candle company and we worked together to get the products ranked high on the site. The product was good. The ranking, the relationship and the work are what sold it.

The documentary I am producing now, Trail with No End, is about a painter whose Trail of Tears mural was commissioned by a university museum in 2000 and then abruptly canceled, with two panels finished. It has never been exhibited. The quality of the work was never the question. The timing, the project and the funding decided what happened to it.

Great products are easier to sell. They are not self-selling. Plan the selling as carefully as the making.

Answered by: Rule 69 (find everyone who can say no, not just the buyer) and Rule 70 (a great pitch fails if the buyer cannot buy).

Phrase 3 · “Every great company starts with a genius in a garage”

What it means. Great businesses come from one brilliant founder with one brilliant idea, working alone.

Why people say it. Because it is a good story. Famous technology companies tell origin stories about a founder, a garage or a dorm room, and a sudden idea. The story is inspiring: it says anyone with talent and a small space can change the world. It also flatters founders, who like to see themselves as the hero.

How it gets overused or misread. People start to believe that they must have the whole idea themselves, that asking for help is weakness, and that partners, advisors and teams are extras added later. Others decide they cannot start because they are not geniuses and do not have a breakthrough idea.

Why it’s dangerous. Origin stories are usually branding, not records. When successful people explain why they succeeded, they tell the story that makes sense to listeners, not a complete account of what happened (Mills, 1940; Nisbett & Wilson, 1977). In my own research on how Oscar-nominated filmmakers explain their choices, I study exactly this gap between the reasons people give and the decisions they actually made. The results are not final yet, but the question itself is a good warning for founders.

Research on creative work points the other way from the genius story. In field studies of professional service firms, creative breakthroughs came when people asked for help, gave help, rethought problems together and supported each other (Hargadon & Bechky, 2006). In a study of Hollywood film actors, those who worked with elite collaborators were more likely to earn Oscar nominations: the quality of your partners shapes how good you look (Rossman et al., 2010).

None of my own businesses was a garage miracle. My biggest Amazon success came from a friendship with a candle maker. My move into intrapreneurship came from a mentor’s advice. My first documentary was self-funded, but it was finished with an investor’s help, and that investor backed the next project too. My film projects are built with co-producers, writers, actors and crews.

The better version: great companies start with someone who can find the right people. Talent matters. Partners, mentors and teams matter as much.

Answered by: Rule 88 (your partner matters as much as your idea), with Rule 8 (owners ask for help and keep the responsibility).

Phrase 4 · “Celebrate failure”

What it means. Treat failure as a good thing, even a badge of honor, because it shows you are taking risks.

Why people say it. Because fear of failure stops people from trying anything new. Organizations that punish every mistake get employees who never take a risk. The phrase is meant to make experimenting safe, and that intention is right.

How it gets overused or misread. “Celebrate failure” slides into “failure is good,” and then into “any failure is fine.” People start to treat a careless, expensive loss the same way as a cheap, well-designed test. Founders tell failure stories as proof of courage without saying what they learned. Teams stop asking why something failed, because asking feels like blame.

Why it’s dangerous. Failure has no value by itself. Its value is what it teaches, and the price you paid to learn it. Pisano (2019) argues that strong innovative cultures tolerate failure but not incompetence: a well-designed test that fails can teach valuable lessons, while sloppy work cannot. Organizations learn from failure only when they notice it, study it carefully and run deliberate experiments (Cannon & Edmondson, 2005).

My most expensive failure was the fashion-technology startup. I spent all my capital on two patents when I should have spent it on software. I do not celebrate that. It cost me the company. What I do value is the specific lesson: spend first on whatever proves customers want the product, and protect the idea once there is something to protect. That lesson has been worth more to me than the patents. But it was a very expensive way to learn it, and a cheaper test would have taught me the same thing.

This book does endorse failing fast, which is different: making small, cheap bets that can fail without harm, so you learn quickly. What it rejects is treating failure as the goal.

The better version: celebrate what the failure taught, and how little it cost.

Answered by: Rule 53 (a failure is worth only what it teaches).

Phrase 5 · “Growth means profit”

What it means. If sales are growing, the business is doing well, and profit will follow.

Why people say it. Because growth is easy to see and exciting to report. Investors, the press and employees all celebrate a rising sales number. For many young businesses, growth really is the first goal, because a business that cannot grow cannot cover its fixed costs. And sometimes growth does bring lower costs per unit.

How it gets overused or misread. Founders chase sales at any price. They add products, markets and channels because each one adds revenue. They give big discounts or spend heavily on ads to win customers. They measure success by the top line and stop looking at what each sale actually leaves behind.

Why it’s dangerous. Revenue and profit are different numbers, and growth can push them in opposite directions. Maytag, a U.S. home-appliance maker, grew its revenue about five times over, from $684 million in 1985 to a peak of $3.4 billion in 1994, while its return on sales fell from 8% to 12% in the 1970s and 1980s to below 1% on average from 1989 to 1995 (Porter, 1996). Blue Apron, a U.S. meal-kit company, had revenue of $795.4 million in 2016 and still lost $54.9 million. In the first three months of 2017, revenue rose 42%, but the company went from a small profit of $3.0 million a year earlier to a loss of $52.2 million (Blue Apron Holdings, Inc., 2017).

I have seen the same pattern at product level. In a margin worksheet I built for a manufacturer, the channel with the best-looking margin at the top, selling online at full price, left the least at the bottom once shipping, warehouse and channel fees were counted. For that product, every additional sale on one big marketplace lost money. Growing sales there would have meant growing losses.

The better version: profitable growth means profit. Before you celebrate a new product, market or channel, follow one sale all the way to your pocket.

Answered by: Rule 62 (bigger is not better when profit gets smaller) and Rule 75 (count every cost between the price and your pocket).

Phrase 6 · “Entrepreneurs love risk”

What it means. People who start businesses are natural gamblers who enjoy danger and bet big.

Why people say it. Because starting a business does involve risk, and many people who never start one feel they could not live with that risk. From the outside, leaving a safe job or putting savings into an idea looks like a love of danger. Famous founders also tell stories of betting everything.

How it gets overused or misread. Young people decide they cannot be entrepreneurs because they are careful. Others decide they must take huge, all-or-nothing bets to prove they are real entrepreneurs: quitting school, borrowing heavily or putting a family’s savings into one idea. Risk becomes a personality to perform instead of something to manage.

Why it’s dangerous. The research does not show that entrepreneurs simply love risk. One combined analysis of earlier studies found entrepreneurs somewhat more willing to take risks than managers (Stewart & Roth, 2001). A later analysis, adding other studies, found the opposite (Miner & Raju, 2004). The honest answer is that the research is mixed and depends on how risk is measured. What successful founders do consistently is something else: they shape their risk.

They decide in advance how much they can afford to lose (Sarasvathy, 2001). They often test a new business while keeping their income: people who started a business while keeping their job had a lower risk of closing it than those who quit first (Raffiee & Feng, 2014). They set stop points before they start. They spread bets so that one shock cannot sink everything.

That is how I moved into film. I did not quit ecommerce and bet everything on a movie. I studied screenwriting while still working, wrote screenplays, produced small projects, and paid for my first documentary myself, at a size I could afford to lose. Only then did I commit fully. Today my film slate has about 30 projects, each run as its own small company, so that no single project can sink the others.

This is the phrase that connects most directly to this book’s core stance. Entrepreneurs accept risk they understand. What they fear, and starve, is chaos they do not understand: the bet with no stop point, the plan with one hidden assumption under everything, the risk nobody counted.

Answered by: Rule 37 (test the new life before you leave the old one) and Rule 38 (decide when you will walk away before you start).

Phrase 7 · “The first mover wins”

What it means. The first company into a new market will dominate it, so speed beats everything.

Why people say it. Because being early can bring real advantages: the first chance at customers, the first relationships with suppliers, time to learn before rivals arrive, and sometimes a brand that becomes the name of the whole category. Famous winners are often remembered as the first, even when they were not (Golder & Tellis, 1993).

How it gets overused or misread. Founders rush a product out before it works, believing that being first matters more than being good. Others give up on a good idea because someone else is already doing it. Investors ask “Who else is doing this?” as if any competitor means the opening is closed.

Why it’s dangerous. Being first is much weaker than the phrase suggests. In a study of about 500 brands in 50 product categories, almost half of the market pioneers, the first companies to sell a new kind of product, had failed, and only about 11% were still market leaders (Golder & Tellis, 1993). Researchers who reviewed first-mover advantages found that they exist in some conditions and not in others; being first also has disadvantages, such as paying to teach the market and making mistakes that later entrants can avoid (Lieberman & Montgomery, 1988).

I have been on both sides. In 2003 I started selling on Amazon, early, before I even had a company. Being early gave me real advantages: I learned the platform’s rules while they were still being written, and I brought brands onto an almost empty digital shelf. But being early was an advantage in learning, not a permanent prize. The rules kept changing, and later sellers kept arriving.

My fashion-technology startup was also early. Twenty years later, the product I imagined still has not been built. Being early was the problem: the market was not ready, and I could not afford to wait for it.

The better version: the first mover learns first. Whether that becomes a win depends on whether you keep improving, can afford the wait, and get the core of the product right.

Answered by: Rule 39 (good bets often look foolish at first) and Rule 96 (some products must not ship until they are right).

Phrase 8 · “You need money to start”

What it means. A real business needs investors, a loan or large savings before it can begin.

Why people say it. Because many businesses do need money: equipment, stock, rent, salaries. Stories about startups often focus on how much they raised. And for people with little money, the phrase can feel like an honest description of an unfair world.

How it gets overused or misread. People wait. They write plans and look for investors before they have a single customer. They believe that without money, there is nothing they can do yet. Some borrow heavily to start something they have never tested.

Why it’s dangerous. Waiting for money often means never starting, or starting with someone else’s money on an untested idea. Experienced founders often do the opposite. Sarasvathy (2001) describes how many begin with the means they already have, who they are, what they know and whom they know, and commit only what they can afford to lose. Sara Blakely started Spanx with $5,000 of her own savings and no outside investors (Briody, 2018).

My own first businesses needed almost no money: a lemonade stand, yard sales, and house sitting and dog walking for neighbors who asked. My landscaping business began with cheap tools and upgraded as the work paid for better ones. When I moved into film, I funded my first documentary myself and did every job on it, and that finished work is what attracted an investor. More recently, a very small budget was enough to attach a writer and several of the key actors to one of my own horror scripts.

The phrase does contain a warning worth keeping. Some businesses really cannot start small. I learned that with a startup that tackled fake online reviews: a network business that needed a lot of money to build, and never raised it. If your idea truly needs large capital before the first sale, know that before you start, and find a smaller first step that tests it.

The better version: you need a first step you can afford.

Answered by: Rule 30 (start with the money you have, not the money you want).

Phrase 9 · “You need industry experience to start”

What it means. Only people who have worked for years in an industry should start a business in it.

Why people say it. Because experience brings real advantages: knowing how the industry works, who the customers and suppliers are, which rules matter and which mistakes to avoid. Many failures come from newcomers who did not know what they did not know. In some fields, such as medicine, building or finance, inexperience can hurt people.

How it gets overused or misread. Young people decide they must spend ten years working for someone else before they can start anything. Experienced people from another field decide a new industry is closed to them. Insiders use the phrase to keep newcomers out.

Why it’s dangerous. Many openings are seen most clearly by outsiders, because insiders accept the industry’s rules without asking why. In 2000, Seth Hopkins became director of the Booth Western Art Museum in Georgia, after telling its founder he knew nothing about art. The museum became a Smithsonian Affiliate in 2006 (Nesmith, 2025), and in 2020 USA Today readers voted it the best art museum in the United States (Hicks, 2020). Research on project teams found that teams did their best work when they had both the freedom to make their own decisions and knowledge from outside the group, especially knowledge from outside the organization and knowledge that was rare (Haas, 2010).

I entered film after twenty years in ecommerce. I did not pretend to be an insider. I learned the language of the industry, earning a master’s degree in producing, and I used the skills I already had: selling, finance, teams and launches. When I made a documentary about women artists in Western American art, I was a complete outsider to that community, and I said so. I sent every person a written brief before the interview and asked all of them the same questions.

The same was true in reverse when I worked inside manufacturers. They hired me precisely because I came from outside their industry and knew digital selling, which they did not.

The better version: you need to learn the industry fast, and to know where your ignorance could hurt someone. In regulated or dangerous fields, partner with someone who holds the license.

Answered by: Rule 47 (not knowing the field is where learning starts).

Phrase 10 · “Pick a hot industry”

What it means. Your success depends mainly on choosing a fast-growing, fashionable industry.

Why people say it. Because some industries really are growing fast, and growth makes it easier to win customers. Investors and the news talk constantly about hot sectors. It feels logical: a rising tide lifts all boats.

How it gets overused or misread. Founders chase whatever is fashionable this year, often with no special skill or advantage in it. They ignore “boring” industries where customers are already paying. And they explain their own plan by pointing to the trend, instead of explaining why their business will win.

Why it’s dangerous. How you run your company matters far more than the industry you choose. A large study combining 18 samples found that the industry explained only about 8% of the differences in profit between businesses, while the business itself explained about 36% (Vanneste, 2017). PACCAR, a U.S. maker of heavy trucks, works in an industry whose sales rise and fall with the economy, and in January 2026 it reported its 87th consecutive year of profit, for 2025 (PACCAR Inc., 2026). A hot industry also attracts many competitors, which can push prices and profits down.

The first company I sold was a commercial laundry, in an industry most people would call boring. It worked because of how we ran it: the customers we chose and the premium we charged.

I also fell for this phrase myself, and caught it. When I first argued for adding a horror film to my slate, my reason was that horror had been growing for ten years. That was a trend, not a reason. When I tested the slate against several possible futures, a better reason appeared: horror is financed and sold by different people than my documentaries, so it would fail under different conditions. The trend had nothing to do with it.

The better version: pick the company you can build. Then choose an industry where that company can win.

Answered by: Rule 12 (the company you build matters more than the industry you pick).
