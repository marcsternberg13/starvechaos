It started with lemonade

My first business was a lemonade stand. I was eight years old.

My parents were holding a yard sale, which Americans also call a garage sale: a family puts things it no longer needs on tables in front of the house, and neighbors and strangers come to look and buy. People were arriving all morning. They walked around the tables, picked things up, talked, waited, and stood in the sun.

I looked at that crowd and had a simple thought. We already had customers. They were already here. Why not sell them something more?

So I set up a lemonade stand at the yard sale. By the end of the day, I had made more money than my parents did.

Pause and guess: What do you think I charged for a cup? How could an eight-year-old make more money than his parents in one day?

That day holds almost everything this book teaches. It is a whole business in miniature, small enough to see all at once. So before we define anything, let us look at what an eight-year-old did, step by step.

What the lemonade stand teaches

Seeing the world differently. Everyone at that yard sale saw the same scene. My parents saw buyers for old furniture. The visitors saw a place to find a bargain. I saw a group of people who were already gathered, already in a buying mood and probably thirsty. Entrepreneurship starts with that kind of looking: noticing what is already there that nobody is using.

Finding a problem worth solving. I did not invent a need. The need was standing in the yard. People spending time outside, walking between tables, want something to drink. The yard sale did not offer one. That gap between what people needed and what was offered was the opening (Rule 20).

Knowing who you are solving it for. My customers were not “everyone.” They were the people at that yard sale, on that day. That is a very clear customer: you can see them, count them and talk to them (Rule 60).

Figuring out what you can do. An eight-year-old cannot open a shop. But I could make lemonade, set up a table, and ask people if they wanted a cup. Good founders start with what they already have and what they can actually do, not with what they wish they had (Rule 30). My parents’ yard sale was the location, the traffic and the trust, all for free (Rule 25).

Selling where buyers already are. I did not have to attract a single customer. I put my offer exactly where people already were, at the moment they were there (Rule 74). That is one of the strongest moves in business, and I made it by accident at eight.

Testing the idea. Without knowing the words, I was testing a hypothesis: people at this yard sale will pay for lemonade. The test was cheap, it was fast, and the answer arrived the same day. If nobody had bought, I would have lost a little lemon, sugar and time. That is a bet small enough to survive (Rules 37 and 42).

Getting feedback. A lemonade stand gives instant feedback. People buy or they walk past. They come back for a second cup or they do not. They comment on the taste, the price or the size. Every cup sold or not sold is information about what to change next (Rule 43).

How it changes you. Making more money than your parents at eight years old changes how you see the world. It taught me that you do not need permission or a big plan to start, that customers are often already standing in front of you, and that a small, quick test tells you more than a long debate.

Measuring success. What counted as success that day? Revenue, the total money taken in, is the easy answer. But a better measure is profit: what was left after paying for the lemons, sugar, cups and ice. Other measures matter too: how many cups sold, how many people came back for another, and whether the stand helped the yard sale or got in its way (Rules 75 and 80).

The whole business in one table

A lemonade stand looks like a children’s game. It is actually a complete business. Every part of a large company is there, just small enough to see. Use the table below as a checklist for any business idea, then replace “lemonade stand” with your own.

Opportunity Where is the unmet need? Thirsty people already gathered at a yard sale Rules 13–21
Customer Who exactly is it for? Visitors to that yard sale, that day Rule 60
Location Where will you sell? Inside the sale, where buyers already were Rule 74
Materials and inventory What do you need, and how much? Lemons, sugar, water, ice, cups, a jug, a table. Too little means lost sales; too much means waste Rule 24
Logistics How do things get where they need to be? Buying supplies, making more lemonade, keeping ice cold, refilling before you run out Rule 90
Operations What is the daily routine? Make, pour, serve, clean, restock, in the same order every time Rule 92
Pricing What will you charge, and how? Per cup? A bigger cup for more? The price must feel easy at a yard sale Rules 81 and 82
Profit margin What is left after costs? Price of a cup minus the lemons, sugar, ice and cup inside it Rules 75 and 76
Marketing How do people find out? A sign, a good spot, a friendly call: “Cold lemonade!” Rule 66
Sales strategy How do you turn interest into a yes? Offer it when people are hot and waiting; let the yard sale bring the crowd Rules 67 and 71
Bulk discounts and freebies Can a deal sell more? Two cups for a lower price; a free taste to start; a free cup for a helper Rule 71
Cash handling How is money kept safe and counted? A box for coins and bills, kept in sight; count at the start and the end Rule 91
Making change Can you take the money customers have? Have coins and small bills ready, or you lose the sale to someone with a large bill Rule 90
Accounting Did you actually make money? Write down what you spent and what you took in; the difference is your profit Rule 75
Returns and complaints What if a customer is unhappy? Too sour, spilled, a bug in the cup: replace it with a smile, and fix the cause Rule 43
Risk What could go wrong? Rain, a slow day, running out of ice, the yard sale ending early Rule 35
Rules and permission Who could say no? Your parents; in many places, local rules on selling food Rules 3 and 69
Partners Who helps, and what do they get? The yard sale brings the customers; does it get a share, a free cup or simply happier visitors? Rule 88
Change What will you do differently next time? More ice, a better sign, a second flavor, a better spot Rule 48

Pause and guess: Which row of this table would an eight-year-old most likely forget? What happens on the day that row goes wrong?

Every business in this book, from a laundry company to a film slate, has these same parts. They only get bigger. When a business is in trouble, the cause is almost always one row of this table that nobody was watching. That is what chaos looks like in a small business. And watching every row, even at a lemonade stand, is how you starve it.

Failure is not chaos

Failure is not chaos. Chaos is chaos.

That sentence is the backbone of this book. Many people think entrepreneurs are fearless, or that they love risk, or that they celebrate failure. None of that is quite true. Entrepreneurs are not afraid of being wrong. They fail fast, they change direction, they spread their bets and they start again. What they are afraid of is something else: chaos that is uncontrolled, not understood and unproductive.

Here is the difference. A failure you planned for, that cost what you could afford, and that taught you which of your beliefs was wrong, is not chaos. It is information. You paid for it, and now you own it.

Chaos is what happens when you do not know why things are going wrong. It is the business where nobody knows the real numbers. The team where nobody knows who decides. The launch where one late file holds up everything and nobody saw it coming. The founder who loses a company and learns the wrong lesson, then repeats the same mistake in the next one. That kind of chaos does not teach you anything. It just eats your money and your time.

So this book asks you to do something specific: starve chaos. Do not feed it with guesses, vague plans, hidden disagreements and numbers you have not checked. Feed your business with evidence, clear choices and small bets you can survive.

Harness it; do not try to remove it

Starving chaos does not mean removing it. You cannot. Markets change, customers surprise you, suppliers move to another state, platforms rewrite their rules, and the future refuses to be predicted. A founder who waits for the world to become calm will wait forever.

The goal is to adapt to chaos and to grow through it. The best entrepreneurs use change as raw material. A new platform is an opening. A rule that blocks you is a map of what to build next. A failure, understood, becomes a better plan. The difference between the entrepreneur and everyone else is not that the entrepreneur faces less chaos. It is that the entrepreneur understands more of it, and turns more of it into something useful.

Three forces

The title of this book names three forces that drive the way entrepreneurs think.

Hunger is the drive to build, to earn, to win, to make something real. Hunger gets you out of bed and into the bazaar before the shop next door opens.

Curiosity is the drive to find out. It turns beliefs into questions and questions into tests. Curiosity asks “Why does everyone do it this way?” and “What would prove me wrong?”

Chaos is the world you build in: uncertain, changing and often unfair. It is not your enemy. It is your working conditions.

These forces are closer than they look. Hunger and curiosity even share machinery in the brain. In a set of experiments, Lau et al. (2020) offered people gambles: accept the risk of an unpleasant electric shock to get food (when they were hungry) or to learn the secret of a magic trick (when they were curious). Curiosity pushed people to gamble in much the same way hunger did. In both cases, saying yes went with more activity in the brain’s reward area, the striatum, starting in its lower (ventral) part, where the nucleus accumbens is. In a real sense, curiosity is a hunger. Both will make people accept risk to get what they want.

That is useful to know, and it is also a warning. The same drives that make entrepreneurs bold can make them reckless. Hunger and curiosity push you toward risk. The rules in this book are how you aim them, so that you take the risks worth taking and starve the chaos that is not.

Each of the 101 rules is tagged with the force or forces it uses most: Hunger, Curiosity or Chaos. The index at the back lists the rules by force.

Two definitions

Entrepreneurship

An entrepreneur is a person who sees a need that is not being met, gathers the resources to meet it, and carries the result, including the risk of failure.

I learned that definition long before I could say it. As a teenager, I ran a landscaping business. When I was 16 and quoted an hourly rate, say $100 an hour, no adult or neighbor would pay it. When I quoted one price for the whole job, they hired me, and on some jobs I earned as much as $300 an hour. Nobody gave me that business. I saw a need, bought the tools, set a price and carried the result. That is entrepreneurship at its simplest (Sections 03 and 09).

Later, as a student at Arizona State University, I asked the university what its biggest contracts were. The answer led me to start a commercial laundry company, which I sold at 23 (Sections 01 and 05). Same pattern, bigger scale: a need, a set of resources, a price and an owner.

Entrepreneurship is not the same as owning a small business, though many entrepreneurs do. It is a way of thinking: you act like an owner of the result, whether or not anyone gave you that job.

Intrapreneurship

An intrapreneur (a term made popular by Pinchot, 1985) is someone who brings a successful entrepreneur’s skills and mindset inside a large organization. When an existing business wants to try something different from what it has been doing successfully, such as a new product line, a new division, a new market or a new way of selling, it brings in an entrepreneur to build it.

I did not plan to become an intrapreneur. After my fashion-technology startup ran out of money, one of my mentors, who was also an advisor and an investor, recommended it. I was already an entrepreneur, so it felt like a strange switch. I took the advice anyway (Section 06).

My first accounts as a consultant were Adobe, ShoreTel and Cisco. Adobe was just moving from selling software on physical discs to selling it by subscription, which was new and poorly understood at the time. I was brought in on the consulting side to help with sales enablement: the messages, materials and tools a sales team needs to sell something new. I understood the subscription offering and its benefits well, and I leaned into value selling, which means selling what the customer gains rather than what the product does. That is intrapreneurship: an entrepreneur’s way of thinking, used to help a large company change.

The proof: an arc in three parts

My own career is the evidence for the rules in this book. It has three parts.

First, the founder years. I have started more than 15 businesses. Some were need-driven: a lemonade stand at my parents’ yard sale when I was eight, yard sales, house sitting and dog walking for neighbors, teenage landscaping. Some were driven by my interests: building sailboats, water features and waterfalls, biofuel, and lighting systems with fiber optics. Some became real companies: early Amazon selling from 2003, a wholesale business I formed in 2005, the commercial laundry company I sold at 23, and a fashion-technology startup with two patents that ran out of money. By the time that startup ended, I had run about ten businesses and sold one.

Second, the builder years. For more than a decade, I worked as an intrapreneur inside established companies: consulting firms, publishers and then five manufacturers across many industries. Manufacturers tend to be systematic and slow to change, and many were not adjusting well to digital selling. That made them a perfect fit for my skills. I built new divisions and digital product lines and sold them through ecommerce and digital marketing. I built digital centers of excellence in Pakistan and in Bangalore, India, and led teams working across more than 15 countries. Through all of those years, I ran my own growth consulting practice on the side. In this book, those employers stay unnamed.

Third, the return. After the last manufacturer, I went back to founding, in a field I had loved since childhood but never worked in full time: film and television. A film producer is the entrepreneur of a film project. I earned a master’s degree in producing at Chapman University, founded Son of a Botanist Productions, funded my first documentary myself, and built a slate that now holds about 30 projects (Sections 04 and 07).

The same skills ran through all three parts. The products changed. The way of thinking did not.

Where this book comes from

This book was written to be taught. It began as the curriculum for a semester-long university course in entrepreneurship, which I teach in person as part of a Fulbright Specialist project. Many of the exercises and examples point to the places where small businesses live in almost any city: markets, bakeries, taxis, phone repair stalls and small workshops. The rules are not local either. They work wherever someone is building something with too little money and too much uncertainty.

Part 5 explains how the book becomes a course.

How to use this book

Read this book like a dictionary, and learn from it like a set of stories.

Look up a rule. Part 2 holds 101 numbered rules in 12 sections. Sections 01 to 07 are about how entrepreneurs think. Sections 08 to 12 are about what they do: sell, manage money, lead people, deliver and build products. Each rule has a short definition and, when needed, a few sub-points. Read one rule at a time. Each one stands on its own.

Read its story. Each section ends with one story from my own life that shows several of its rules at work. When a rule appears in that story, its entry points you to the part of the story where it appears. Some rules appear in more than one section; the second listing simply points back to the first.

Watch for the phrases. Part 3 explains ten common sayings that sound wise but mislead founders, such as “The customer is always right” and “Entrepreneurs love risk.” Each one points back to the rules that answer it.

Use the forces. If you want to work on one force, such as curiosity, use the force index at the back to find every rule tagged with it.

When research or another company appears in this book, it is there as evidence, credited to its original authors. The stories are mine.

Now, let us starve some chaos.
