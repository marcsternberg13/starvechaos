Primer

Most of what you start will not work the way you planned. Some of it will fail completely. That is not a special risk for unlucky people. It is the normal pattern of a building life.

This book’s core stance matters most here: failure is not chaos. A failure you understand, that cost what you could afford and taught you something, is part of the work. What entrepreneurs fight is the chaos that comes after a failure they do not understand: blaming the wrong thing, repeating the same mistake, or quitting the next attempt because the last one hurt.

Resilience is not the same as stubbornness. It is the ability to separate what died from what survived, learn the right lesson, and start again, sometimes in a completely different way. The eight rules in this section show how.

The main story is the fashion-technology startup I founded in my twenties. It earned two patents. It also ran out of money and closed.

Key terms

  • Failure. A result that does not meet the goal. Some failures are cheap and useful; others are expensive and teach nothing.
  • Setback. A loss that slows or stops a project without ending your ability to build.
  • Pivot. A deliberate change of direction based on what you learned (Ries, 2011).
  • Grit. Steady effort toward a long-term goal, through setbacks (Duckworth et al., 2007).
  • Gatekeeper. A person or organization that decides what gets in: an investor, a buyer, a curator, a regulator.
  • Intrapreneur. An entrepreneur who builds something new inside an existing organization (Pinchot, 1985; see Part 1).

The Rules

51 · You can lose the company and keep the maker Hunger · Chaos

A business can fail while the skills, taste and drive that built it survive. People who build again learn to separate who they are from the project that just ended.

  • Grief is real: founders often grieve a failed business. Managing that grief is what makes learning from it possible (Shepherd, 2003).
  • Not an excuse: separating yourself from the failure does not mean ignoring your part in it.

→ See What died and what did not.

52 · Rejection often means early, not wrong Chaos · Hunger

A “no” from a gatekeeper is often about timing, not quality. Keep the work, use the rejection, and give the world time to catch up. Before you decide you are early, ask several people why they said no and look for the same reason repeated.

53 · A failure is worth only what it teaches Chaos · Curiosity

Do not celebrate failure itself. Celebrate cheap, honest lessons. Treat each mistake as evidence about which of your assumptions was wrong, then update.

  • Cheap and useful vs. expensive and careless: a small planned test that reveals a wrong assumption is valuable. A careless, costly flop is just a loss (Cannon & Edmondson, 2005; Pisano, 2019; see Part 3, Phrase 4).

→ See Where the money went.

54 · After a crisis, first agree what you are for Chaos · Hunger

Recovery rarely needs new people or more money first. It needs everyone to agree again on the main task and on who decides what.

55 · Answer a setback by changing the game, not trying harder Hunger · Chaos

After a hard hit, the comeback usually comes from changing how you compete, not from repeating the old plan with more effort. If the plan was sound and only the execution failed, better effort may be enough. Know which case you are in.

→ See The advice that changed the game.

56 · Build excellence first; recognition comes later Hunger

People shut out of an industry often win by quietly building excellence first. Awards and funding reveal success; they do not create it. At some point, you must also show the work.

  • Deliberate practice: focused, effortful practice aimed at getting better (Ericsson et al., 1993).

57 · Most of your work will lose; make the next one Chaos · Hunger

Careers move like roller coasters, not ladders. Even the largest companies fail often, so the next attempt matters more than any single loss. Each new attempt should use what the last one taught, or you are only repeating the same loss more often.

  • Equal-odds rule: the number of hits rises with the number of attempts (Simonton, 1997).

→ See Ten businesses, one sale.

58 · Expect the system to push back on new ideas Chaos · Hunger

Rules, money and habits were built for the old way of doing things, so new ideas meet friction. Plan for it, listen to it, and try to turn blockers into partners. Some rules protect customers; not every rule is an obstacle.

The Story: Two Patents and an Empty Bank Account

Where the idea came from

During my freshman year at Arizona State University, before I started the laundry company, I worked part-time as a salesperson in a high-end fashion store.

That job gave me an idea: a digital wardrobe. It would be software that customers paid for by subscription, plus a service around it. Later, once I knew commercial laundry from the inside, I also saw how the two could connect. I describe the product itself, and what it was for, in Section 12.

The idea was strong enough to earn two patents.

San Francisco

I founded a tech startup around the idea. I started it in Scottsdale, Arizona, and then moved it to San Francisco, because that was where the investors, engineers and early-stage companies were.

In San Francisco I worked constantly. I met with venture capitalists, investors who fund young, fast-growing companies, again and again while I pitched. I built a prototype, an early working version of the product. I looked for co-founders. And I tested the idea with retailers to see if they would use it.

Pause and guess: What do you think investors said about a product nobody had built yet?

Where the money went

Here is the mistake I want every student to learn from. I spent all my capital on legal protection: the patents. I should have spent it on software and development.

The patents felt like progress. They were official. They protected the idea. But a patent does not build a product, find a customer or prove that anyone will pay. The software would have done those things, and I had no money left to build it.

That failure taught me something specific (Rule 53). My hidden assumption was that the idea itself was the valuable thing, so the idea needed protecting first. The real valuable thing was evidence that customers wanted it, and only a working product could produce that evidence. The lesson was not “patents are bad.” It was “spend first on whatever proves the business, and protect it once there is something to protect.”

It was an expensive lesson. I have never forgotten it.

Too early

In the end, I abandoned the idea because it was too early. The tools, the customers and the money were not ready for it.

Twenty years later, the product I imagined still has not been built. That is a strange kind of proof. It suggests the idea was not simply wrong. It was early, and I could not fund it long enough to wait for the world to catch up (Rule 52). Investors have an old saying that being early is the same as being wrong. It is true if you cannot pay for the wait.

What died and what did not

When the startup ended, I had run about ten businesses and sold one. The startup needed a lot of capital, and I was running out of money.

The company died. I did not (Rule 51). What survived was everything that had built it: the ability to see a gap, to design a product, to pitch to investors, to test with customers, and to keep going after hearing no. Those skills went with me into every job and every company since.

Separating the two was not instant.

The advice that changed the game

One of my mentors, who was also an advisor and an investor, gave me advice I did not expect. The advice was to become an intrapreneur: someone who brings an entrepreneur’s skills and mindset inside a large organization to build something new for it.

I was an entrepreneur. This was a different game. But I could see the point. I could keep trying to raise money for one risky startup after another, with the same lack of capital each time. Or I could change how I competed (Rule 55). Inside a large company, the money already existed. What those companies lacked was someone who could build a new product line, division or way of selling. That was exactly what I knew how to do.

So I took the advice and pivoted. I joined a consulting company in the Bay Area to learn how big companies work. My first accounts were Adobe, ShoreTel and Cisco. At Adobe, which was moving from selling software on CDs to selling it by subscription, I worked on the sales side of that shift, helping the company explain and sell something new. Over the next years I built new digital businesses inside publishers and then inside five manufacturers. I tell that part of the arc in Part 1 and in Sections 10 and 11.

Trying harder at the same game would have meant another startup with no money. Changing the game gave me a decade of building at a scale I could never have funded myself, and it made me a much better entrepreneur when I came back to founding.

Ten businesses, one sale

Ten businesses and one sale is not a record most people would put on a poster. But it is a normal record for someone who builds (Rule 57). Most attempts lose. The ones that win usually come after many that did not.

I have kept going. I have now started more than 15 businesses. Not all of them worked. Recently, I founded a startup that tackled fake online reviews, a real and very large problem. It never raised investment, so it could not build the network it needed. I restarted the idea with a new team under a new name. That failed too. A network business needs a lot of money and time, and I did not have enough of either for that idea. So I moved on.

The lesson from all of them is the same: make the next one, and make it smarter than the last.

For a founder in your city

When something you built fails, write two lists. The first list is what died: the money, the company, the name. The second list is what you still have: your skills, your contacts, your lessons, your reputation for trying. Start the next thing using only the second list. And before you spend your first money, ask: what will prove that customers want this? Spend there first.
