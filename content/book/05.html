Primer

Curiosity is the second force in this book’s title, and in business it has a practical job. It turns beliefs into questions, and questions into tests. An entrepreneur who is curious does not ask “Am I right?” They ask “How would I find out if I am wrong?”

This matters because most of what you believe about a new business is a guess: who will buy, what they will pay, why they choose you. Curiosity is how you replace guesses with evidence before the guesses become expensive. The nine rules in this section are about asking better questions, trusting behavior over opinion, and designing tests that can actually prove you wrong.

The main story begins with one simple question I asked my university, Arizona State: what are your biggest contracts? The answer, and the rule that came with it, changed my plans.

Key terms

  • Hypothesis. A guess stated so clearly that a test can prove it wrong. “Students will pay for laundry pickup” is a hypothesis.
  • Test. A small, cheap action that produces evidence about a hypothesis.
  • Evidence. What people actually did, counted or recorded. Opinions are weak evidence; behavior is strong evidence.
  • Stop rule. The result, written before a test, that will mean you were wrong.
  • Open question. A question that cannot be answered with yes or no, such as “What happened?” or “Tell me more.”
  • Pure inquiry. Asking because you truly do not know, not to steer the answer (Schein, 1999).

The Rules

Rule 41 · Ask what would have to be true — see Section 04. Here, use it to test: find the condition you doubt most, and test it first.

42 · Decide what would prove you wrong before you look Curiosity · Hunger

Before you run a test, write down the result that would mean you were wrong, and what you will do if you get it. If you wait until after you see the results, hope will bend how you read them (Kunda, 1990).

→ See What I would do differently.

43 · Believe what customers did, not the reasons they give Curiosity

People’s explanations for their choices are often stories they tell others, not a record of how they decided (Nisbett & Wilson, 1977). Trust what customers actually do more than what they say they will do.

  • Ask about the past, not the future: “Tell me about the last time you…” beats “Would you…?” For a similar approach, see Fitzpatrick (2013).

→ See Forty dollars a bag.

44 · Speed up the test, slow down the thinking Curiosity · Chaos

Fast experiments are good at fixing small things. Only stepping back to question your main assumptions tells you whether the whole plan is right. Build a short, regular pause for that into every cycle.

  • Single-loop learning: fixing actions inside your current assumptions.
  • Double-loop learning: questioning the assumptions themselves (Argyris, 1976).

45 · Your instincts are often wrong, so let tests decide Curiosity

Even experienced people guess wrong about what customers want (Kohavi & Thomke, 2017). When you can, compare two versions and let the result decide, not the opinion of the most senior or most confident person.

→ See What a year taught me.

46 · A test only counts where the customer really is Curiosity · Chaos

A product that works in your workshop can still fail in real life. Put rough versions into real conditions early, because most problems live outside the thing you built (also in Section 12).

  • Sticky information: knowledge about how customers use a product stays where the customer is and is hard to carry back to your desk (von Hippel, 1994).

47 · Not knowing the field is where learning starts Curiosity · Hunger

You do not need years of experience in an industry before you start. You can learn a new field quickly by asking people outside your circle and reading widely. In fields where mistakes hurt people, such as medicine or finance, partner with someone licensed.

48 · Your strategy is a guess, not a promise to defend Curiosity · Chaos

Treat your plan as a hypothesis (McGrath & MacMillan, 1995). When results do not match the plan, that gap is information, and the leader’s job is to change course openly. Change because the evidence is clear, not because one day was bad.

49 · The people easiest to reach will mislead you Curiosity · Chaos

Friends, fans and people who quickly say yes are not your market. Look for strangers, keep a record of who said no, and be careful with easy agreement. A large sample of the wrong people gives a more confident wrong answer.

50 · Start with what you do not know Curiosity

Separate what you know, what you only assume and what you truly do not know. Then ask sincere, open questions about the unknown part (Schein, 1999).

→ See The question.

The Story: The Question I Asked My University

The question

By the time I was a student at Arizona State University, I was already selling on Amazon. One of my best products was a laundry detergent, and through that detergent company I had started to learn how the laundry business worked.

I wanted to know where the real money was. So I did something simple that most students never do. I asked the university a direct question: what are your biggest contracts?

I did not go in with a pitch. I went in with a question (Rule 50). I knew a little about laundry. I assumed a lot about how a big university spends its money. And I truly did not know where the large contracts were. So I asked.

Pause and guess: Who would you ask, at a university or hospital in your city, to find out where the big contracts are? What exact words would you use?

Food first, and no

The first answer was food. Feeding tens of thousands of students is a huge contract.

I did not want food.

Pause and guess: Why might a founder say no to the biggest contract on the list?

This is a small moment, but it matters. A good answer to your question is not always an answer you must act on. Curiosity collects information. Judgment decides what to do with it.

Forty dollars a bag

The second-biggest contract was laundry. It covered all the dorms and the residences around the campus, serving about 100,000 students. And students were already paying for it: personal laundry was being done at $40 a bag.

That number told me more than any survey could have (Rule 43). I did not need to ask students whether they would pay for laundry service. They were already paying, and paying a lot. Their behavior was the evidence. If I had asked students “Would you pay $40 for a bag of laundry?”, many would have laughed and said no. But they were doing it.

When you look for a business, look for money that is already moving. What people pay for today is much stronger evidence than what they tell you they might pay for tomorrow.

The one-year rule

I wanted to bid on that contract. The university said no, not yet. I could not bid until I had created my own laundry company and run it for a full year.

At the time, that felt like a wall. Looking back, it was one of the most useful rules anyone ever gave me. It forced me to test my idea in the real world, with real customers, before I could reach for the big prize.

So I went and did it. I started the company.

Pause and guess: When someone tells you “not yet,” is that a wall or a map?

What a year taught me

To survive that first year, I needed customers. The university was not one of them yet. So I went after commercial customers: high-end hotels, spas, gyms and offices that cared about the environment and social responsibility. I tell that part of the story, and how I could charge three times my competitors’ price per pound, in Section 01.

Here is the learning lesson. I had started with an instinct: the university contract was the prize. A year of running the company tested that instinct, and the result surprised me (Rule 45).

I liked commercial work better than residential work. The commercial customers valued what we did and paid a premium for it. The university contract would have been residential laundry, student bags at scale. The real test, a year of running the business, showed me that the work I had been chasing was not the work I wanted.

No amount of thinking at a desk would have told me that. I had to run the business to find out.

The contract I stopped needing

By the end of that period, we were handling a truckload of laundry every day. At that volume, I no longer needed the university contract. I chose commercial over residential, kept building commercial accounts, and began bidding on large hotels instead.

The contract that started the whole company was a contract I never took. That is not a failure. It is what learning looks like when you let the evidence change your plan (Rule 48, and Rule 52 in Section 06).

I sold the company when I was 23.

What I would do differently

One thing I did not do, and would do today, is write down in advance what would prove me wrong (Rule 42).

When I started, my test was really “Can I survive a year so I can bid?” A better test would have had numbers in it. For example: “I am wrong about commercial laundry if, after six months, I have fewer than a certain number of accounts or I cannot charge more than my competitors.” Writing it down first would have made the year a clearer experiment, and it would have stopped me from reading the results the way I hoped to read them.

Pause and guess: What number, written down before the year started, would have told me I was wrong?

Your turn to ask

You can ask the same question I asked, almost anywhere. Go to a large organization in your city: a university, a hospital, a factory, a city office. Ask, politely and sincerely: what do you spend the most money on, and who supplies it today?

Then listen for three things. Where is money already moving? Who is already paying, and how much? And what rule stands between you and that money, and what would you have to build to meet it?
