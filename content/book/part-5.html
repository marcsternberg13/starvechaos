The course in one paragraph

This book becomes a semester-long course called The Starve Chaos Curriculum, first built for a host university abroad. I first taught it in person as a Fulbright Specialist project, “U.S. Entrepreneurship, Phase 1: Business Development.” The course runs for 16 weeks, with two two-hour sessions a week: 32 sessions in all. When more than one group of students takes the course, each group gets the same 32 sessions. There is no final exam. Grades come from participation (15%), quizzes (15%), weekly team work (25%), the midpoint pitch (10%) and the final presentation (35%). Students work in teams of four or five on one real business problem for the whole semester and finish by presenting a business idea and plan. This part describes the curriculum, written so the course runs the same way all semester. Full session plans, slides, assignments and surveys for instructors are at starvechaos.com.

The shape of a session

Every session follows the same rhythm, so students always know what comes next.

  1. Warm-up (about 15 minutes). A short quiz on the last session, then one word from the section on the screen, such as Chaos, Opportunity or Margin. Students define it in their own words before anything is taught.
  2. The story and the ideas (about 20 minutes). I tell part of the section’s story from my own life and stop to ask what the class thinks happened next. Students name the rules they see. Questions are welcome.
  3. Class exercise (about 35 minutes). In the classroom, the class applies two or three rules to a real local business: a bakery, a bazaar stall, a taxi service, a phone repair shop, a tutoring center or a cotton workshop.
  4. Group work (about 25 minutes, after a five-minute break). Each team uses the same rules on its own project and adds one piece to its Problem Map.
  5. Quiz and exit question (about 15 minutes). Teams share what they found. Each student takes a short graded quiz on the session and answers one exit question on their phone before leaving.

In a section week, the first session carries the story and the main rules. The second goes deeper into the rules, adds one of the Ten Phrases from Part 3, and gives the teams more time on their project.

Sixteen weeks, 32 sessions

The semester has three parts. Week 1 opens the course. Weeks 2 to 8 teach Sections 01 to 07, one section a week, and end with a midpoint in Week 9. Weeks 10 to 14 teach Sections 08 to 12. The last two weeks turn each team’s work into a plan and a presentation.

Week 1: the lemonade stand

Session 1 opens with the lemonade stand from Part 1, before any rule is taught. It is the simplest possible business, and every student can picture it. A one-page handout carries the story and a blank version of the component table.

  1. One word: Entrepreneurship. Students define it in their own words before hearing the story.
  2. The story. I tell how, at eight, I sold lemonade to the customers already at my parents’ yard sale and made more money than they did.
  3. Unpack it together. The class answers the questions in “What the lemonade stand teaches”: How did I see the opportunity? Who was the customer? What was the hypothesis, and how was it tested? What feedback came back? How would we measure success?
  4. The component table. In teams, students fill in the blank version of “The whole business in one table” for a local version of the same idea: a tea or water stand at the busiest market in town, a snack table outside a university exam hall, or a street snack sold at a busy taxi stand. Each team must name a price, a cost per item, a way to make change and one deal (a bulk price or a freebie).
  5. Exit question. “Where in our city are customers already gathered, waiting, with nothing to buy?”

Session 2 forms the teams and introduces the key words of the course, using a one-page English glossary. Each team chooses the problem it will work on all semester.

The same table returns at the end of the course. In the final presentations, each team’s idea must answer every row.

The team project: a Problem Map

In Week 1, each team chooses one real local business or one real problem that people in their city face. They keep the same problem for the whole semester.

The heart of the project is a Problem Map: a one-page picture of the customer’s real problem. Understanding the problem is one of the hardest and most important things a founder does, so the map asks students to find:

  • The customer: who exactly has the problem.
  • The pain: what hurts, how often and how much.
  • The current options: what people do today, including competitors, substitutes and workarounds, and what they simply avoid.
  • Willingness to pay: evidence, not guesses, that someone will pay to solve it (Rules 43 and 82).
  • The assumption to test: the one thing that must be true for a solution to work, and how to test it cheaply (Rules 41 and 42).

The group work in each session adds one piece to the map. By Week 9, every team has a map and a first idea that solves the problem.

The midpoint: Week 9

The first session of Week 9 is a full class of spoken idea pitches. Each team stands up, without slides, and has three minutes to explain its problem, its customer and its idea, followed by four minutes of feedback from the class and the instructor. The pitch is scored out of 20. In the second session, teams use that feedback to revise the idea before the second half of the book begins.

The pitch follows the same method I use with my own film projects: conceptualize the idea, package it simply, and pitch it to the people who would have to say yes. In this method, the pitch is the cheapest test. A team that cannot get a room of classmates to lean in has learned something important for almost no cost. That is failing fast on purpose.

The final presentations: Weeks 15 and 16

In Week 15, teams build their plan and their slide deck, then rehearse and get feedback. Week 16 is only for final presentations: each team presents its business idea and plan with PowerPoint slides to the class and invited guests: 12 required slides, eight minutes plus four minutes of questions, scored out of 100.

Each presentation must answer four questions: Who is it for? What problem does it solve, and what evidence do you have? What is the smallest first step you can afford? What would prove you wrong? And it must answer every row of the component table from Week 1.

Starve Chaos as the backbone

Two simple tools keep the core stance in every session.

The chaos log. Each team keeps a running log of the chaos they meet in their project: a customer who did not answer, a number they could not find, a team disagreement, an assumption that turned out wrong. For each entry, they write one line: was this failure we understand, or chaos we do not? And what will we do about it?

Chaos cards. At random moments, the instructor hands a team a card that changes their situation, such as “Your main supplier has stopped making the product,” “A new rule bans selling on the street,” or “Your best customer wants half the price.” The team has ten minutes to respond using the rules. The cards make the point that chaos is the normal working condition, and the skill is responding to it.

Guest speakers

Guest speakers from business join by video call. Their visits are short slots spread across the semester, not all on the same day, plus optional 30-minute teaching sessions. Every guest slot has backup content ready, so a session never depends on one person’s connection or schedule. That is Rule 95 in action: plan for the delay you cannot see yet.

Built for a projector and phones

The course is taught in person, in plain English with no idioms, and without translations. Slides go on a projector. Most students will use the course materials on their phones, so everything on starvechaos.com is built mobile first. Readings are short. Quizzes and exit questions open directly on a phone at starvechaos.com, with a code given in class. Each book section and its teaching materials are released when the class reaches them, with a password shared in class.

Using this book without the course

You do not need a classroom to use this structure. A reader working alone, or a small group of founders, can follow the same rhythm: one section a week, in two sittings. Define the word first. Read the story. Pick two rules. Apply them to one real business. Write one answer to the exit question: what will I do differently this week?
